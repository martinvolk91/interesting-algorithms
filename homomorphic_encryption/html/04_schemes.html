04 · The schemes: five "generations" of FHE#

← 03 How it works · index · next: 05 Core techniques →

Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) schemes are usually grouped into generations. Most scheme names are the authors' initials: DGHV (van Dijk–Gentry–Halevi–Vaikuntanathan), BV (Brakerski–Vaikuntanathan), BGV (Brakerski–Gentry–Vaikuntanathan), BFV (Brakerski/Fan–Vercauteren), GSW (Gentry–Sahai–Waters), CGGI (Chillotti–Gama–Georgieva–Izabachène) and CKKS (Cheon–Kim–Kim–Song). FHEW is the Ducas–Micciancio scheme ("Fastest Homomorphic Encryption in the West"), and TFHE (torus fully homomorphic encryption) is the CGGI scheme. Generation 2 is built on ring learning with errors (RLWE) and packs many values into one ciphertext, single instruction, multiple data (SIMD) style.

The grouping is loose, and the later generations did not replace the earlier ones. BGV/BFV, TFHE and CKKS are all actively used and developed, because they are good at different things.

Gen 1 (2009–10)  Gentry (ideal lattices), DGHV (integers)        proof of concept, impractical
Gen 2 (2011–12)  BV, BGV, BFV  (RLWE, leveled, SIMD)              exact modular arithmetic, batched
Gen 3 (2013–16)  GSW → FHEW → TFHE / CGGI                         fast bootstrapping, bits / LUTs
Gen 4 (2017)     CKKS                                             approximate real-number arithmetic
"Gen 5" (2025)   Gentry–Lee (GL) matrix FHE                       native encrypted matrix multiplication

Generation 1: feasibility#

  • Gentry 2009: ideal lattices, a "somewhat" HE scheme, plus squashing (making the decryption circuit shallow) and bootstrapping. The landmark result, but impractical.
  • DGHV 2010 (van Dijk, Gentry, Halevi, Vaikuntanathan): the same blueprint over the integers. A ciphertext is c = m + 2r + p·q for a secret odd integer p. Decryption is (c mod p) mod 2. Conceptually simple and great for teaching (see deep dives), but keys are huge.
  • The first implementation (Gentry–Halevi 2011) needed about 30 minutes per bootstrap and gigabyte-scale keys.

Generation 2: BGV and BFV (exact arithmetic over Z_t)#

Papers. Brakerski–Vaikuntanathan 2011 (BV: relinearization, (R)LWE). Brakerski–Gentry–Vaikuntanathan 2012 (BGV: modulus switching, leveled FHE without bootstrapping). Brakerski 2012 and Fan–Vercauteren 2012 (BFV: scale-invariant).

Plaintext space. Polynomials in \(R_t = \mathbb{Z}_t[X]/(X^N+1)\). With a prime \(t \equiv 1 \pmod{2N}\), batching turns this into N independent slots of integers mod t, operated on SIMD-style (ch. 05).

The difference between the two is where the message sits:

BGV BFV
Decryption equation c₀ + c₁s = m + t·e (mod q) c₀ + c₁s = Δ·m + e (mod q), Δ = q/t
Message lives in low bits (noise is a multiple of t) high bits (noise below it)
Noise control modulus switching after each mult (required) scale-invariant: multiply by t/q and round; modulus switching optional
Typical sweet spot larger t, deep circuits small t, simpler parameter management

Kim–Polyakov–Zucca (2021) showed that with modern residue number system (RNS) optimizations the two have similar performance. BGV tends to win for larger plaintext moduli, BFV for small ones.

Deep dive: deep_dives/bfv_pir.md builds BFV step by step (code/toy_bfv.py) and uses it for a caller-ID PIR demo (code/pir_caller_id.py).

Good for: exact integer workloads such as counting, private information retrieval (PIR), private set intersection, database lookups, genomics, and exact statistics. Apple's Live Caller ID and Microsoft's Password Monitor are BFV.

Weak at: non-polynomial functions (comparison, division) and bootstrapping, which is slow and complicated (digit extraction). Most BGV/BFV deployments are leveled.

Generation 3: GSW, FHEW, TFHE (CGGI)#

GSW (Gentry–Sahai–Waters 2013): the "approximate eigenvector" scheme#

A ciphertext is a matrix C such that, for the secret vector v,

\[C \cdot v \approx m \cdot v \qquad (\text{$v$ is an approximate eigenvector, $m$ the eigenvalue})\]

So C₁ + C₂ encrypts m₁ + m₂ and C₁ · C₂ encrypts m₁ · m₂, using plain matrix arithmetic with no evaluation key and no relinearization. With the gadget decomposition G⁻¹, the product's noise grows asymmetrically: it is roughly poly(n)·e₂ + m₂·e₁. One factor's noise is scaled by a fixed poly(n); the other's only by the small message. Chaining many multiplications in the right order (e.g. a branching program) therefore grows noise only linearly. This asymmetry is what makes the fast bootstrapping of FHEW and TFHE possible. GSW ciphertexts are big, so GSW is rarely used directly. Its idea lives on as the GGSW (general GSW, over rings) ciphertexts in TFHE bootstrapping keys.

FHEW (Ducas–Micciancio 2015)#

Encrypt single bits with plain LWE. Bootstrap after every NAND (not-and) logic gate, with an RLWE/GSW accumulator that performs the decryption's rounding step "in the exponent" of X. It was the first bootstrapping under one second.

TFHE / CGGI (Chillotti–Gama–Georgieva–Izabachène 2016)#

Formalized over the real torus \(\mathbb{T} = \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\), implemented with 32/64-bit integers. It has three ciphertext types:

Type What it is Role
LWE (a, b) with b = ⟨a,s⟩ + e + m carries the data
GLWE (general learning with errors; here RLWE) polynomial version accumulator during bootstrapping
GGSW (GSW over rings) a matrix of GLWEs bootstrapping key: encryptions of the secret-key bits

The external product GGSW × GLWE → GLWE is cheap, and it gives the CMux (controlled multiplexer) gate (encrypted if-then-else), which blind rotation uses (ch. 03 §8). Gate bootstrapping took under 0.1 s in the 2016 paper, and about 13 ms a year later.

Deep dive: deep_dives/tfhe.md walks through programmable bootstrapping step by step, with a runnable toy implementation (code/toy_tfhe.py).

Programmable bootstrapping (Chillotti–Joye–Paillier 2021, Zama) generalizes it: each bootstrap applies an arbitrary lookup table to a small (≈ 1–8 bit) encrypted integer. Zama's TFHE-rs builds 8- to 256-bit integers out of radix "blocks" of 2-bit messages plus carries, which gives encrypted u64 arithmetic, comparisons and branching-free control flow.

Good for: non-linear and branchy logic (comparisons, max, ReLU, sorting), arbitrary functions on small integers, smart-contract-style logic, and low-latency single operations. Noise never accumulates, so there is no depth planning.

Weak at: throughput. Each ciphertext holds one small value and every operation pays a bootstrap, so large linear algebra is much slower than with CKKS/BFV batching.

Generation 4: CKKS (Cheon–Kim–Kim–Song 2017)#

Approximate arithmetic on real or complex numbers. It is also called HEAAN, after the original library.

Deep dive: deep_dives/ckks.md covers encoding, rescaling and bootstrapping step by step, with a runnable toy implementation (code/toy_ckks.py).

Encoding. A vector \(z \in \mathbb{C}^{N/2}\) is mapped to a polynomial by the inverse canonical embedding (evaluating at the complex roots of \(X^N+1\); an inverse fast Fourier transform, FFT), multiplied by a scale Δ (e.g. 2⁴⁰), and rounded to integer coefficients. One ciphertext holds N/2 complex slots, e.g. 32 768 values at N = 2¹⁶.

Arithmetic. Slot-wise add and multiply, rescale after each multiplication (drop a prime, ch. 03 §7), and rotate slots with Galois keys. Noise is rounding error, and precision is managed like floating point.

The difference from BFV is the missing rounding step. Both put the message above the noise, but BFV rounds the noise away when it decrypts, while CKKS keeps it as the low digits of the answer:

Bootstrapping. Cheon–Han–Kim–Kim–Song (2018). ModRaise → CoeffToSlot → EvalMod (sine approximation) → SlotToCoeff. It costs roughly 10–18 levels and is approximate. On central processing units (CPUs) it takes seconds per (fully packed) ciphertext. Graphics processing unit (GPU) implementations bring it to the tens to hundreds of milliseconds; FIDESlib reports ≥ 70× over OpenFHE optimized with Advanced Vector Extensions (AVX). Because it refreshes tens of thousands of values at once, the amortized cost per value is tiny.

Good for: machine learning inference and training, statistics, linear algebra, signal processing. Anything that tolerates ~20–40 bits of precision. It dominates encrypted neural-network research, including the work on encrypted large language models (LLMs) in ch. 07.

Weak at: exact results, and non-polynomial functions, which need polynomial approximations (ch. 05). Its decryption leaks noise, so it needs noise flooding before results are shared (IND-CPA-D: security when the attacker also sees decryptions, ch. 06).

Recent twist (2024–2026): "discrete CKKS". Bae–Cheon–Kim–Stehlé (Eurocrypt 2024) showed how to bootstrap bits. Bae–Kim–Stehlé–Suvanto (Asiacrypt 2024) bootstrapped small integers with functional (LUT) bootstrapping, at 3.75 ms amortized per 8-bit LUT. Alexandru, Kim and Polyakov did general functional bootstrapping in CKKS. These blur the old line "CKKS for reals, TFHE for discrete".

"Generation 5": Gentry–Lee (GL), FHE for matrix arithmetic (2025)#

  • Paper: C. Gentry, Y. Lee, "Fully Homomorphic Encryption for Matrix Arithmetic", IACR ePrint 2025/1935. The "5th generation" branding comes from a DESILO press release (March 2026); treat it as marketing, not a community consensus.
  • Idea: an RLWE-based scheme whose plaintexts are batched matrices, over the complex numbers or the integers. Encrypted matrix multiplication reduces to four matrix multiplications of ciphertext components. It needs no slot-to-coefficient conversion and no ring switching, and it natively supports transpose, conjugate transpose and matrix rotations.
  • Why it matters: in CKKS, a matrix product needs many rotations (key switches) and careful packing. Under GL it becomes plaintext-style matrix multiplication, which maps directly onto GPU tensor cores and BLAS.
  • Bootstrapping for GL: Crockett, Gentry, Kim, Lee and Lee (CRYPTO 2026, ePrint 2026/956). In their proof of concept, linear transformations are 20.1 % of bootstrapping time, versus 54.9–71.7 % in prior CKKS bootstrapping.
  • Status: very new. Library support, independent benchmarks and cryptanalysis of its specific structure are still emerging. It is one to watch.

Scheme switching: using several at once#

Real workloads mix linear algebra (good in CKKS) with non-linear decisions (good in TFHE). Scheme switching converts ciphertexts between them, roughly by extracting LWE ciphertexts from RLWE slots and packing LWE ciphertexts back into RLWE via key switching:

  • CHIMERA (Boura–Gama–Georgieva–Jetchev 2018/2020), PEGASUS (Lu et al. 2021).
  • OpenFHE ships CKKS ↔ FHEW switching.
  • Hybrid CKKS/TFHE encrypted LLM decoding (e.g. ROSETTA, arXiv 2609.16915).

A security note: Santato–Zanotto 2026 (ePrint 2026/285) showed PEGASUS-style switching is not IND-CPA-D secure and demonstrated key recovery against OpenFHE's CKKS→FHEW path (ch. 06).

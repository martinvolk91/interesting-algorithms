07 · State of the art (as of October 2026)#

← 06 Security · index · next: 08 Ecosystem and applications →

How to read this chapter. It reflects a literature and news search on 2026-10-29. Many 2026 items are preprints (IACR ePrint, arXiv) or vendor and press claims, and are marked as such. Numbers are as reported by the authors. Most full papers were not re-checked line by line; abstracts and summaries were used. Treat this as a map of where to look, not as verified benchmarks.

0. The picture in ten bullets#

  1. The scheme landscape is stable but converging. BGV/BFV, CKKS and TFHE remain the big three. CKKS is absorbing TFHE's strength (functional bootstrapping on discrete data), and TFHE is absorbing batching ideas. A new matrix-native scheme (Gentry–Lee) appeared in late 2025.
  2. Bootstrapping is still the bottleneck and the main research target. CRYPTO 2026 has results that cut FHEW/TFHE bootstrapping from 500+ sequential NTT layers to 3.
  3. Hardware has arrived as silicon. Intel's Heracles FHE accelerator (DARPA DPRIVE) was presented at ISSCC 2026 with ~10³× speedups over a 24-core Xeon on core operations. Startups (Niobium, Cornami, Optalysys, Fabric) are building chips.
  4. GPUs are the practical accelerator today. TFHE bootstraps take under 1 ms on an H100. GPU CKKS libraries (FIDESlib, Cheddar, Phantom) report 1–2 orders of magnitude over optimized CPU code.
  5. Encrypted LLM inference works but isn't practical. 21 CKKS frameworks have run end-to-end private inference up to 8B-parameter models, but the best are still about 10⁴× slower than plaintext.
  6. Security research has shifted from "is LWE hard?" to "how do real systems leak?": the IND-CPA-D attacks on CKKS, BFV, TFHE and scheme switching (ch. 06).
  7. Standardization is close. ISO/IEC 28033 (FHE) Parts 2 (BGV/BFV), 3 (CKKS) and 4 (CGGI/TFHE) reached Draft International Standard ballots in early 2026.
  8. Compilers are maturing. Google's MLIR-based HEIR is positioning itself as the common layer across schemes, libraries and hardware.
  9. Production deployments are real but narrow: private lookups (Apple, Microsoft) and confidential blockchain tokens (Zama). General encrypted backends are not shipping.
  10. The remaining gaps: latency, memory (keys in the GB range), developer ergonomics, verifiability, and secure-by-default parameters for shared decryptions.

1. Bootstrapping research#

FHEW / TFHE#

  • Jain, Lin, Liu, Saha. "New Techniques for Fast and Shallow FHE Bootstrapping and Beyond" (CRYPTO 2026; ePrint 2026/1730)
    • With a sparse, small-norm LWE secret (Hamming weight h), blind-rotation cost drops from Õ(n²) to Õ(n·h) Z_q-multiplications. That gives a 4.5–7.5× speedup over OpenFHE's gate and functional bootstrapping.
    • With a new structured-secret RLWE variant and a relinearization-free BV-style multiplication, the sequential NTT/INTT depth drops to 3 (versus 500+ in standard FHEW/TFHE) at similar total work. The authors expect large latency wins on GPUs and other parallel hardware.
    • Includes a security analysis of the new assumption (worst-case to average-case and search-to-decision reductions, concrete attacks). It is a new assumption, so expect follow-up cryptanalysis.
  • GPU TFHE: bootstrapping latency reported as < 1 ms on an NVIDIA H100 and about 189 000 bootstraps/s across 8 H100s (Zama; vendor figures via secondary sources).
  • Encrypted LSTM via PBS (ePrint 2026/1091): 211 µs amortized per bootstrap, and 5.2 s inference for a 128-token sequence.

CKKS#

  • MRFHE: Mixed-Radix FHE with Better Batch Bootstrapping (Cheon et al., ePrint 2026/853): recasts the DFT steps of CKKS bootstrapping as batched matrix multiplications with a constant number of key switches per ciphertext. Implemented in Lattigo.
  • Discrete / functional CKKS bootstrapping (2024–2025): bootstrapping bits (Bae–Cheon–Kim–Stehlé, Eurocrypt 2024, 2024/767), small integers with LUTs (Bae–Kim–Stehlé–Suvanto, Asiacrypt 2024, 2024/1637): 3.75 ms amortized per 8-bit LUT over 2¹² ciphertexts, single-threaded at 128-bit security, general functional bootstrapping (Alexandru–Kim–Polyakov, 2024/1623), modular reduction (2024/1638), and high-precision discrete CKKS for noise flooding (Choe–Kim–Stehlé–Suvanto, 2025/1786). Together these turn CKKS into a credible "do everything" scheme.
  • GPU CKKS: FIDESlib reports ≥ 70× faster bootstrapping than AVX-optimized OpenFHE (secondary source). Cheddar and Phantom are other high-performance GPU libraries.

Matrix FHE#

  • Crockett, Gentry, Kim, Lee, Lee. "Efficient Bootstrapping in FHE for Matrix Arithmetic" (CRYPTO 2026; ePrint 2026/956): bootstrapping for the Gentry–Lee scheme. Linear transformations take 20.1 % of bootstrapping time, versus 54.9–71.7 % in prior CKKS work.

2. New schemes#

  • Gentry & Lee, "Fully Homomorphic Encryption for Matrix Arithmetic" (ePrint 2025/1935, Oct 2025). RLWE-based, with native batched matrix multiplication (four ciphertext-component matrix products), transpose and rotation, over complex numbers and integers. DESILO markets it as the "5th generation" ("GL scheme") and partners with Cornami on hardware. It was presented at FHE.org 2026 (Taipei). See ch. 04.

3. Encrypted AI and LLM inference#

Status (per the 2026 SoK): CKKS-based private LLM inference is algorithmically feasible but operationally impractical for interactive use.

  • SoK: Private LLM Inference using Approximate HE. Al Badawi, Alexandru, Polyakov, Vaikuntanathan (ePrint 2026/935).
    • 21 CKKS frameworks have shown end-to-end private LLM inference, up to 8B parameters (Llama-3-8B).
    • Only 5 of 21 are model-preserving: they run the unmodified model with the whole prompt encrypted, with no retraining or architecture substitution.
    • Polynomial approximations keep downstream accuracy high from BERT-Tiny to Llama-3-8B.
    • The runtime gap is about four orders of magnitude versus plaintext.
    • It releases POLARIS, an open-source model-preserving framework (whole BERT family, CPU and GPU), and proposes a "Private LLM Card" reporting standard.
  • ELLMo (ePrint 2026/198): packing- and depth-aware transformer inference, a new matrix multiplication with fewer rotations, and up to 46 % fewer bootstraps in non-linear layers.
  • Error-propagation-aware scale design (ePrint 2026/2242): tunes CKKS scales per layer to cut bootstrapping in LLM inference.
  • Hybrid CKKS/TFHE decoding: ROSETTA (arXiv 2609.16915). CKKS for the linear layers, TFHE for exact non-linear steps.
  • Encrypted state-space models for long contexts as a transformer alternative: FESC (arXiv 2608.17442).
  • Encrypted recommendation models: HE-LRM (arXiv 2506.18150).
  • Other end-to-end systems often cited: THOR, Powerformer, Tricycle (per the EDBT 2026 tutorial by Lim, Agrawal and El Abbadi, "Private LLM Inference with Homomorphic Encryption").
  • GPU encrypted GPT-2-class inference: about 200× over CPU baselines (ICML 2025, as cited by secondary sources). That is still far from interactive.

Reality check: for private LLM inference today, confidential GPUs and TEEs are the practical option. FHE wins where trust in hardware vendors is unacceptable, or for small and medium models (logistic regression, trees, small NNs), which run in seconds under CKKS or Concrete ML.

4. Hardware acceleration#

Why FHE needs custom hardware: the work is dominated by NTTs, modular multiplication over wide RNS limbs, automorphisms (data permutations) and, above all, memory bandwidth: key-switching keys are hundreds of MB to GB and are streamed for every rotation and relinearization.

Deep dive: deep_dives/hardware.md measures where the time goes in our toys, implements the NTT and RNS, models key switching at bootstrapping size (memory-bound by ~3–18×), and walks through GPUs, F1, BTS, CraterLake, ARK, SHARP and Heracles.

Academic ASIC lineage (simulated designs)#

F1 (MICRO 2021) → BTS and CraterLake (ISCA 2022) → ARK (MICRO 2022) → SHARP (ISCA 2023) → systolic designs such as Osiris (arXiv 2408.09593). These showed 1000×+ speedups over CPU in simulation and set the architecture template: many NTT lanes, huge on-chip SRAM, HBM, and on-the-fly key generation.

DARPA DPRIVE (2021–) and Intel Heracles#

  • Intel Heracles, presented at ISSCC (Feb 2026) after about five years of DPRIVE work. It is a dedicated FHE accelerator, not a general-purpose CPU, with 48 GB HBM3.
    • 1 074–5 547× faster than a 24-core Sapphire Rapids Xeon across seven key FHE operations (some reports give the upper bound as 1 547×; the sources conflict).
    • Demo: an encrypted query went from 15 ms (Xeon) to 14 µs.
    • No announced commercial plans. It is a research prototype.

Startups#

  • Niobium Microsystems (a DPRIVE spin-out): raised $23M, and reportedly has an 8 nm chip deal with Samsung Foundry.
  • Cornami: reconfigurable compute fabric, partnered with DESILO for GL-scheme acceleration.
  • Optalysys: photonic (optical) FHE acceleration.
  • Fabric Cryptography: a cryptography-focused processor.

GPUs (available now)#

TFHE-rs CUDA backend, FIDESlib, Cheddar, Phantom, HEaaN-GPU. For batched CKKS and PBS-heavy TFHE, GPUs give roughly 10–100× over CPU (vendor and secondary figures).

6. Compilers and developer tooling#

  • HEIR (Google): MLIR-based universal HE compiler (arXiv 2508.11095). Python frontend (@compile() decorator), OpenFHE and Lattigo backends. Google promoted it in Aug 2026 for converting pretrained models to encrypted inference, with demos on recommendation, fraud detection, intrusion detection and hotword models (secondary reports).
  • Concrete / Concrete ML (Zama), Orion (PyTorch → CKKS), Fhelipe.
  • The trend: programmers describe the computation, and the compiler chooses the scheme, parameters, packing, bootstrap placement and backend (CPU, GPU or ASIC).

7. Security research (summary; details in ch. 06)#

  • The IND-CPA-D attack line now covers CKKS (2021), average-case noise estimation in libraries (USENIX Sec 2024), exact schemes via decryption failures (CCS 2024), scheme switching (2026/285) and BFV in OpenFHE (2026/2546).
  • Noise-flooding countermeasures revisited (PKC 2025). High-precision discrete CKKS makes flooding affordable (2025/1786).
  • Lattice assumptions themselves are holding (the 2024 quantum-LWE claim was retracted). New assumptions (structured-secret RLWE in 2026/1730, GL's matrix structure) need scrutiny.

8. Industry and deployments#

  • Apple: Live Caller ID Lookup (iOS 18, BFV-based PIR), Enhanced Visual Search (BFV + differential privacy + anonymization network), and the open-source swift-homomorphic-encryption.
  • Microsoft: Edge Password Monitor (BFV in SEAL, inside a PSI protocol).
  • Zama: the Zama Protocol went live on Ethereum mainnet on 31 Dec 2025 (TFHE + threshold MPC for confidential tokens, ERC-7984). It runs at about 20 TPS on CPU. The CEO claims 1 000 confidential transfers/s on GPUs (self-reported, without methodology). Roadmap targets of 10k TPS (2028) and 100k TPS (2030) are aspirational.
  • Google: HEIR, Private Join and Compute (additive HE).
  • Duality, CryptoLab (HEaaN), DESILO, Inpher, Tune Insight: enterprise analytics, healthcare, finance (AML), and genomics pilots.
  • Jeremy Kun's FHE in production keeps a skeptical list of what is truly deployed versus pilots and rumours.

9. Open problems worth following#

  1. Bootstrapping latency: from ms toward µs (TFHE), and from seconds toward ms (CKKS).
  2. Memory: evaluation keys of GBs. On-the-fly key generation, key compression.
  3. Secure by default under shared decryptions (IND-CPA-D) without large precision loss.
  4. Verifiable FHE: efficient proofs that the server computed correctly.
  5. Multi-party FHE at scale: threshold decryption networks, key resharing, robustness.
  6. Non-linear functions in batched schemes: softmax, LayerNorm, comparisons.
  7. Standard benchmarks: comparable numbers across libraries and hardware (the SoK's "Private LLM Card" is one attempt).
  8. Cryptanalysis of new structures: sparse/structured secrets, GL matrix encodings, circular security.
  9. Usability: compilers that make FHE as easy as writing NumPy.

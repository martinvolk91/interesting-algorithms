P9 · Bootstrapping: resetting the noise#

← P8 Relinearization and modulus switching · index · next: 01 Introduction →

Every scheme in P7 and P8 carries noise, and every operation makes it bigger. Modulus switching slows the growth, but the budget is fixed when the keys are made, and once it is spent the ciphertext cannot be computed on any more. Bootstrapping resets the noise and so removes the limit. It is the "F" in FHE: Craig Gentry's 2009 breakthrough, and still where most FHE running time and most FHE research go.

In this chapter

  • See the noise wall in a running toy and say exactly when decryption fails.
  • Write decryption as a function of the key, with the ciphertext as constants.
  • Follow Gentry's idea step by step, and explain why the old noise does not survive it.
  • State its two conditions, bootstrappability and circular security, and map Gentry's jewelry-store analogy onto them.
  • Run a TFHE blind rotation by hand on a 4-coefficient polynomial, and say what GSW, GGSW and a CMux contribute.
  • Describe how TFHE, CKKS and BGV/BFV bootstrap, and roughly what it costs.
  • Use programmable bootstrapping to compute a non-linear function while cleaning the noise.
  • Read chapters 01–03 of the main notes with every term already familiar (§16).

1. The noise wall#

Recall from P7: an LWE ciphertext (a, b) has phase \(\varphi = b - \langle a, s\rangle = \Delta m + e\), and decryption rounds \(\varphi / \Delta\). That is correct while \(\lvert e\rvert < \Delta/2\). Additions add noises, a public constant k multiplies the noise by k (P7), and a ciphertext product multiplies noises (P8). Modulus switching (P8) slows the growth, but each level is spent once: a leveled scheme with L levels handles depth L and no more. The noise budget is the distance to the limit in bits, \(\log_2(\Delta/2) - \log_2\lvert e\rvert\).

The script for this chapter (../code/primer/p9_bootstrapping.py) uses the toy TFHE of the main notes: Q = 2^32 and 2-bit messages. TFHE keeps one extra padding bit above the message, always 0, so the circle is cut into \(2^3 = 8\) sectors rather than 4, and \(\Delta = Q/8 = 2^{29}\) (not \(Q/4 = 2^{30}\)); the limit is \(2^{28}\). The padding bit keeps every phase in the first half of the circle, which TFHE's bootstrapping needs because of the negacyclic sign flip (shown in §5, "Blind rotation by hand"; see also the TFHE deep dive §1.2). The script encrypts x = 1 and repeats x ← 3·x (plaintext × ciphertext), which computes \(3^k \bmod 4\) at step k. Each step triples the noise, spending \(\log_2 3 \approx 1.58\) bits of budget:

 step | expected 3^k mod 4 | decrypts to | |noise|  | budget left
    0 |                  1 |           1 | 2^  9.8 |  18.2 bits  ok
    1 |                  3 |           3 | 2^ 11.3 |  16.7 bits  ok
   ...
   10 |                  1 |           1 | 2^ 25.6 |   2.4 bits  ok
   11 |                  3 |           3 | 2^ 27.2 |   0.8 bits  ok
   12 |                  1 |           0 | 2^ 28.8 |  -0.8 bits  FAILED

18.2 / 1.58 ≈ 11.5 steps, and step 12 is the first wrong answer: the noise wall.

The obvious way through the wall is to decrypt and re-encrypt, since a fresh ciphertext has small noise. But only the key owner can decrypt, and sending ciphertexts back to the client for every refresh defeats the purpose. Bootstrapping is "decrypt and re-encrypt" done by the server, under encryption, without the key.

2. Gentry's jewelry store#

Gentry explained the idea with a story in his 2010 Communications of the ACM article "Computing arbitrary functions of encrypted data". Paraphrased:

The glovebox. Alice owns a jewelry store but does not trust her workers with the gold and diamonds. She locks the materials in a transparent, impenetrable box with built-in gloves; only she has the key. A worker assembles a ring through the gloves, and Alice unlocks the box and takes it out. This is the "glovebox" of 01 §1.

The defect. Her boxes are defective: after about a minute the gloves stiffen and become useless, and her finest pieces take an hour. But the boxes have a one-way slot, like a mailbox, and are flexible enough to be pushed inside one another.

The eureka moment. After dreaming of a dragon eating its own tail, Alice gives the worker box 1 with the materials, plus box 2 with the key to box 1 locked inside, box 3 with the key to box 2, and so on. The worker uses box 1 until its gloves stiffen, pushes box 1 into box 2, and with box 2's fresh gloves unlocks box 1 and keeps working on the half-made piece. When box 2's gloves stiffen, box 2 goes into box 3, and so on. This works as long as unlocking a box plus a little real work fits into one minute of glove time.

In the story In the math
a locked box with materials inside a ciphertext of m
working through the gloves homomorphic operations
gloves stiffen after a minute the noise budget runs out
key to box i, locked inside box i+1 the bootstrapping key: key i encrypted under key i+1
unlocking box i from inside box i+1 evaluating decryption homomorphically
fresh gloves of box i+1 the reset noise after bootstrapping
unlocking + a little work within a minute bootstrappability (§6)
grease that makes the lock easier squashing (§6)
a key locked inside the box it opens circular security (§7)

Gentry carries the story into circular security too. Suppose a key is locked inside the very box it opens: could the gloves and the key open the box from inside? If so, he writes, "it would be a strange lock"; likewise, schemes that become insecure in this setting tend to be contrived. The story makes it plausible, but the math still needs an extra assumption there (§7). His epilogue sums up 2010 performance: the thick gloves slow every step down so much that Alice keeps the boxes for her most valuable diamonds only.

3. Decryption is just a function#

Decryption has two inputs. Usually we fix the key and vary the ciphertext. Gentry's first move is the opposite: fix the ciphertext c and treat the key as the input, \(D_c(x) = \mathrm{Dec}(x, c)\). For LWE,

\[D_{(a,b)}(x) = \mathrm{Round}\big(b - \langle a, x\rangle \bmod q\big),\]

with a linear part (the inner product with the key) and a non-linear part (the rounding). The ciphertext's numbers a and b are now constants. For a 1-bit message (\(\Delta = q/2\)), \(\mathrm{Round}(\varphi) = 1\) if \(q/4 \le \varphi < 3q/4\), else 0.

Worked example. Take q = 16, n = 3 (\(\Delta = 8\)) and the key s = (1, 0, 1). Encrypt m = 1 with mask a = (5, 11, 14) and noise e = 2: \(\langle a, s\rangle = 5 + 14 = 19 \equiv 3\), so \(b = 3 + 8 + 2 = 13\). Fixing this ciphertext gives a function of three key bits:

\[D_c(x_1, x_2, x_3) = \mathrm{Round}\big(13 - 5x_1 - 11x_2 - 14x_3 \bmod 16\big), \qquad \mathrm{Round}(\varphi) = [\,4 \le \varphi < 12\,]\]
key candidate x \(\varphi\) = 13 − ⟨a, x⟩ mod 16 \(D_c(x)\)
(0, 0, 0) 13 0
(0, 0, 1) 15 0
(0, 1, 0) 2 0
(0, 1, 1) 4 1
(1, 0, 0) 8 1
(1, 0, 1), the real key 10 = 8 + 2 1 = m
(1, 1, 0) 13 0
(1, 1, 1) 15 0

Three things to notice:

  1. \(D_c\) is an ordinary Boolean function of n bits, so by P6 it is a circuit of XOR and AND gates. Another ciphertext gives a circuit of the same shape with other constants.
  2. The ciphertext is not an input of this circuit. It is part of its description.
  3. The noise e = 2 hides inside the constant 13. At the real key it gives \(\varphi = 10\), which rounds to 1, and any \(\lvert e\rvert < 4\) lands in \([4, 12)\) with the same result. The rounding is where noise dies.

4. Gentry's idea step by step#

  1. Client, once: encrypt every key bit and send the result with the other evaluation keys (P8). This is the bootstrapping key \(\mathit{bk} = \big(\mathrm{Enc}(s_1), \dots, \mathrm{Enc}(s_n)\big)\). TFHE's LWE key is binary (every entry is 0 or 1), so each entry is a key bit; a key with larger entries would be encrypted bit by bit.
  2. Server: take the noisy ciphertext c and write down the circuit \(D_c\), with the numbers of c built in as constants.
  3. Server: evaluate \(D_c\) homomorphically, gate by gate, on the encrypted key bits, then carry on computing with the result.

Step 3 gives

\[\mathrm{Eval}\big(D_c,\ \mathrm{Enc}(s_1), \dots, \mathrm{Enc}(s_n)\big) = \mathrm{Enc}\big(D_c(s_1, \dots, s_n)\big) = \mathrm{Enc}(m).\]

That is just the correctness rule of HE (P6 §1, 01 §2) for the function \(f = D_c\) on the inputs \(s_1, \dots, s_n\).

 CLIENT (once)                        SERVER
 bk = Enc(s1), ..., Enc(sn)  ------>  stored with the other evaluation keys

                                      c = (a, b): Enc(m), noise e close to Δ/2
                                        | (1) write decryption with c built in:
                                        v     D_c(x) = Round(b - <a, x>)   a, b = constants
                                      circuit D_c
                                        | (2) run D_c on bk with homomorphic + and x
                                        v
                                      c' = Eval(D_c, bk) = Enc(D_c(s)) = Enc(m)
                                           noise from bk and the gates of D_c, not from e

The server never sees s, only encryptions of its bits, which look random (IND-CPA, P5), provided that publishing them is safe (§7). Gentry describes the message as briefly doubly encrypted: m sits inside c under the old key, and the computation that peels off that inner layer runs inside a new encryption, like Alice unlocking box i from inside box i+1.

Why the old noise does not carry over#

  • Noise only comes from ciphertext inputs. A homomorphic computation starts with the noise of the ciphertexts it is fed and grows it with each operation. Public constants have no noise of their own: adding one leaves the noise unchanged, multiplying by one scales the noise already there.
  • The noisy ciphertext is not an input. The inputs of \(\mathrm{Eval}(D_c, \mathit{bk})\) are the entries of bk, freshly made with small noise. The old a and b are constants.
  • The old noise becomes data, and the data gets rounded. Under encryption, the circuit computes the value \(\varphi = \Delta m + e\) and rounds it to m, exactly like the truth table above. The old e disappears as in ordinary decryption.

So the output noise is "the noise of bk, grown by the gates of \(D_c\)": fixed by the parameters, independent of e. Demo 2 of the script shows it: inputs with noise \(2^{9.8}\), \(2^{17.7}\) and \(2^{27.2}\) come out at \(2^{16.9}\), \(2^{14.8}\) and \(2^{17.6}\), a random scatter with no trend. (Because the output noise no longer reflects the computation, bootstrapping also helps hide what was computed: circuit privacy, 06 §4.) Two consequences often surprise people:

  • The output can be noisier than a fresh ciphertext. Demo 2 bootstraps a fresh ciphertext and raises its noise from \(2^{9.8}\) to \(2^{16.9}\). Bootstrapping resets the noise to a fixed level; what matters is that the level leaves room for work (6–8 more ×3 steps in the toy).
  • A ciphertext that is already wrong cannot be saved. If \(\lvert e\rvert \ge \Delta/2\), the rounding inside \(D_c\) picks the wrong message, and the bootstrap returns a clean encryption of that wrong message (demo 2, step 12). The server must bootstrap before the wall. It cannot see the noise, so it tracks a public upper bound (demo 3).

(In the CACM article the bits of c are first encrypted afresh rather than used as constants. Fresh encryptions have small noise, so the argument is the same.)

5. A tiny bootstrap in pseudo-code#

The whole idea for a 1-bit LWE ciphertext: ordinary decryption, then the same program with every key bit replaced by its encryption.

# Ordinary decryption of a 1-bit LWE ciphertext (a, b), with Δ = q/2
def dec(s, a, b):
    phi = (b - sum(a[i] * s[i] for i in range(n))) % q   # linear part: inner product
    return 1 if q // 4 <= phi < 3 * q // 4 else 0         # non-linear part: rounding

# Bootstrapping: s[i] replaced by bk[i] = Enc'(s[i]), operations made homomorphic
def bootstrap(bk, a, b):          # a, b: the noisy ciphertext, used only as public numbers
    acc = trivial(b)              # Enc'(b) with zero mask and zero noise
    for i in range(n):
        acc = acc - a[i] * bk[i]  # public constant times ciphertext, then a subtraction
    # acc now encrypts phi = b - <a, s> = Δ·m + e; the server cannot see phi
    return hom_round(acc)         # "is phi in the middle half?", under encryption
  • trivial(b) is the ciphertext with mask 0, noise 0 and phase b. Any public value can be "encrypted" this way.
  • a[i] * bk[i] would multiply the noise of bk[i] by up to q. Real schemes avoid that with P8's gadget decomposition. In the simplest version bk holds \(\mathrm{Enc}'(2^j s_i)\) for each bit position j, and \(a_i s_i = \sum_j a_{i,j} \cdot 2^j s_i\) with bits \(a_{i,j}\), so the loop only adds key entries and the noise grows additively. TFHE gets the same effect from GGSW ciphertexts (below).
  • Enc' must hold the phase, a number mod q. TFHE first rounds a and b to a small modulus 2N (a modulus switch, P8) so that the phase becomes a small exponent of X; why 2N is explained below.
  • hom_round is the hard part: the linear part is cheap, the rounding is not. Each scheme family's bootstrapping is a different answer to "how do you round under encryption?" (§8).

In TFHE every line has a direct counterpart (details in 03 §8 and the TFHE deep dive):

Pseudo-code TFHE step
reduce a, b to a small modulus mod switch Q → 2N: the phase becomes an exponent of X
acc = trivial(b) trivial encryption of \(X^{-\tilde b} \cdot v(X)\), with the test polynomial v
acc = acc - a[i] * bk[i] blind rotation: a CMux with GGSW(s_i) multiplies by \(X^{\tilde a_i s_i}\); the subtraction happens in the exponent
hom_round(acc) v holds the rounded value for every phase; sample extraction reads coefficient 0
(result under the big key S) key switching back to the small key s

The two subsections below unpack the TFHE column: first the ciphertext type that makes the loop cheap, then the loop itself on a tiny example.

GSW and GGSW: multiplying by an encrypted bit#

GSW (Gentry, Sahai and Waters, 2013) encrypts a small value m, such as a bit, not as a pair but as a matrix C. Write \(\hat s = (1, s)\) for P8's key vector. A GSW ciphertext satisfies

\[\hat s \, C = m \cdot \hat s \, G + e\]

where G is the gadget matrix (the powers \(1, w, w^2, \dots\) of P8 §6, once for each entry of \(\hat s\)) and e is a row of small errors. Column by column, C is a stack of ordinary \((c_0, c_1)\) ciphertexts of \(m w^i\) and of \(m w^i s\), the same structure as P8's relinearization key. The GSW paper states the same idea with a key-derived vector that is an approximate eigenvector of the matrix C, with the message as its eigenvalue.

To multiply, write \(C_2\) in digits, \(G^{-1}(C_2)\) (a matrix of small entries with \(G \cdot G^{-1}(C_2) = C_2\)), and take a matrix product:

\[\hat s \, C_1 \, G^{-1}(C_2) = (m_1 \hat s G + e_1)\, G^{-1}(C_2) = m_1 \, \hat s \, C_2 + e_1 G^{-1}(C_2) = m_1 m_2 \, \hat s G + m_1 e_2 + e_1 G^{-1}(C_2)\]

The key vector stays \((1, s)\): no \(s^2\) term appears, so no relinearization is needed. The new noise \(m_1 e_2 + e_1 G^{-1}(C_2)\) is asymmetric: \(e_2\) is multiplied by the message \(m_1\) (0 or 1 for a bit) and \(e_1\) only by small digits. If the left factor is always a fresh ciphertext, each product adds a fixed amount of noise instead of multiplying it.

TFHE uses the ring version. GLWE is RLWE, possibly with several mask polynomials instead of one (the toy uses one, which is plain RLWE); its secret key S is a polynomial with N binary coefficients. GGSW is GSW over GLWE ("a GLWE times the gadget"). The product of a GGSW encryption of a bit with an ordinary GLWE ciphertext, the external product, is a GLWE encryption of bit × message, again with only additive noise growth. The TFHE deep dive builds both in Python (§3.1 for the external product).

Blind rotation by hand#

How does TFHE round under encryption? By turning the phase into a power of X.

Exponents of X live mod 2N. In \(\mathbb{Z}_Q[X]/(X^N + 1)\) we have \(X^N = -1\), so \(X^{2N} = 1\), and multiplying by \(X^k\) depends only on \(k \bmod 2N\). That is why TFHE first rounds a and b from modulus Q to modulus 2N (a modulus switch, P8 §8): the rounded phase \(\tilde\varphi = \tilde b - \langle \tilde a, s \rangle \bmod 2N\) can then be used as an exponent. The rounding adds a little noise of its own.

The accumulator holds \(X^{-\tilde\varphi} \cdot v(X)\). It starts as the trivial encryption of \(X^{-\tilde b} \cdot v(X)\). Subtracting \(\tilde a_i s_i\) from the value in the exponent means multiplying by \(X^{\tilde a_i s_i}\), so after all n steps the exponent is \(-\tilde b + \sum_i \tilde a_i s_i = -\tilde\varphi\). This is the pseudo-code's acc = acc - a[i] * bk[i], with the subtraction done in the exponent.

Each step is a select. A key bit is 0 or 1, so multiplying by \(X^{\tilde a_i s_i}\) means "rotate by \(\tilde a_i\) if \(s_i = 1\), else leave it":

\[\mathrm{ACC} \leftarrow \mathrm{ACC} + s_i \cdot \big(X^{\tilde a_i} \cdot \mathrm{ACC} - \mathrm{ACC}\big)\]

That is P6's select \(y + c\,(x - y)\) with an encrypted condition \(s_i\): the CMux. The product of \(s_i\), given as a GGSW ciphertext in the bootstrapping key, with the GLWE ciphertext \(X^{\tilde a_i} \mathrm{ACC} - \mathrm{ACC}\) is an external product, so over the n steps the noise only adds up.

Rotating reads the table. Multiplying by \(X^{-k}\) moves coefficient k of v down to position 0. With \(N = 4\) and \(v = 10 + 11X + 12X^2 + 13X^3\), P7's negacyclic rule gives

\[X^{-2} \cdot v = 12 + 13X - 10X^2 - 11X^3, \qquad X^{-5} \cdot v = -11 - 12X - 13X^2 + 10X^3\]

(in this ring \(X^{-2} = -X^2\) and \(X^{-5} = X^3\)). For \(k = 2\) the constant coefficient is \(v_2\), the table entry for phase 2. For \(k = 5 \ge N\) it is \(-v_1\): phases in the second half of the circle read the table negated. TFHE's padding bit (§1) keeps the phase in the first half, where the table reads as written.

Sample extraction. After the rotation, the constant coefficient of the accumulator encrypts \(v_{\tilde\varphi}\). Coefficient 0 of a GLWE ciphertext \((A, B)\) is already an LWE ciphertext: its phase is \(B_0 - (A \cdot S)_0\), and \((A \cdot S)_0\) is the inner product of S with row 0 of the negacyclic matrix of A, that is \((A_0, -A_{N-1}, \dots, -A_1)\) (P7 §8; for P7's mask \(a = (86, 3, 59, 21)\) this row is \((86, 76, 38, 94)\)). So \(\big((A_0, -A_{N-1}, \dots, -A_1),\ B_0\big)\) is an LWE ciphertext under the coefficient vector of S, and extracting it costs nothing. A key switch (P8 §7) then brings it back under the small key s.

6. Bootstrappability and squashing#

Bootstrapping helps only if the scheme can evaluate \(D_c\) without overflowing and with room to spare. Gentry calls a scheme bootstrappable if it can evaluate its own decryption circuit augmented by one gate. The gate matters: a refresh that ends at the limit gains nothing. So merge a gate into the refresh. For ciphertexts \(c_1, c_2\) and an AND gate, evaluate

\[D^{\wedge}_{c_1, c_2}(x) = D_{c_1}(x) \cdot D_{c_2}(x)\]

on bk, which yields \(\mathrm{Enc}(m_1 m_2)\) with reset noise. Do this for every gate of a circuit and noise never accumulates from gate to gate, so any depth works. TFHE's gate bootstrapping is exactly this: a NAND is one addition plus one bootstrap. In depth terms, with L the multiplicative depth a scheme can evaluate before overflowing:

\[\mathrm{depth}(\mathrm{Dec}) + 1 \le L.\]

Gentry's somewhat homomorphic schemes missed this by a little. The CACM article uses the integer scheme of van Dijk, Gentry, Halevi and Vaikuntanathan (DGHV): a bit m is encrypted as \(c = m + 2r + p\,k\), a near-multiple of a secret odd number p, with small noise r and a large random k. Decryption is \((c \bmod p) \bmod 2\). For example, with \(p = 101\), \(c = 1 + 2 \cdot 3 + 101 \cdot 57 = 5764\) gives \(c \bmod p = 7\), odd, so \(m = 1\). But computing \(c \bmod p = c - p \cdot \mathrm{round}(c/p)\) needs \(c/p\) to about \(\log_2 p\) bits of precision: decryption multiplies the long numbers c and 1/p, and the bits of that product are polynomials of too high a degree for the scheme. His fix was squashing: the public key also carries a "hint", a large set of numbers in which a hidden sparse subset adds up to 1/p. The encrypter (or the server) uses it to do part of the decryption work in advance, and decryption shrinks to adding a handful of numbers, which is low-degree. The price was a second hardness assumption (sparse subset sum) and larger ciphertexts. In the story, it is the grease Alice puts on each lock before the box goes into the next one.

Modern schemes build bootstrappability in from the start. TFHE's blind rotation uses GGSW products whose noise grows additively over its n steps, so depth is no obstacle; the cost moves into time. CKKS, BGV and BFV choose parameters big enough (\(N = 2^{16}\), moduli of well over a thousand bits) that bootstrapping's own levels fit with levels to spare.

7. Circular security#

The bootstrapping key encrypts the secret key under itself. Is that safe? IND-CPA (P5) does not say: in its game the attacker picks messages without knowing the key, so a message that is the key is outside the game. Artificial IND-CPA-secure schemes can be built that break completely once \(\mathrm{Enc}_s(s)\) is published. Gentry gives two options:

  • A chain of keys. Keys \(s_1, \dots, s_{L+1}\), with \(s_i\) published encrypted under \(s_{i+1}\); each bootstrap moves the ciphertext one key along (Alice's boxes 1, 2, 3, …). No key encrypts itself, so IND-CPA suffices. But there is one key per level, key generation grows with the depth, and the depth is fixed in advance: a leveled scheme again.
  • One key, encrypted under itself. Constant-size keys and unbounded depth: true FHE. It needs an extra assumption, circular security: publishing \(\mathrm{Enc}_s(s)\) reveals nothing useful. Gentry noted that no attack was known for his or most other schemes, but that such security is typically hard to prove. That is still true of LWE-based FHE in 2026 (02 §7), and every FHE library that bootstraps takes this option.

TFHE's two keys still form a cycle. TFHE has a small LWE key s (dimension n, about 700–1000 in practice) for data and a large GLWE key S (a polynomial of size N, often 2048) for the accumulator. Its bootstrapping key \(\mathrm{GGSW}_S(s_i)\) encrypts s under S; its key-switching key \(\mathrm{LWE}_s(S_j)\) encrypts S under s. A bootstrap goes from s to S and back. No key encrypts itself, but the two encrypt each other: a key cycle of length 2, which needs the same kind of assumption. (Two lines of keygen() in ../code/toy_tfhe.py build it.) The two keys are there for speed, not to avoid circularity; breaking the cycle would take a fresh key per bootstrap, the chain again.

RLWE schemes meet the same issue in another form: relinearization and rotation keys (P8) encrypt functions of s, such as \(s^2\), under s. Brakerski and Vaikuntanathan's original relinearization used a different key per level to avoid circularity; libraries use one key.

8. How real schemes bootstrap#

Each family follows §5: the linear part under encryption, then the rounding under encryption. The compact version is 03 §8.

  • TFHE / FHEW: a bootstrap after every gate. The phase becomes an exponent of X in \(\mathbb{Z}_Q[X]/(X^N + 1)\); blind rotation turns the test polynomial by it without learning it, and the rounding is a free table look-up. Each ciphertext holds one small value (about 1–8 bits). Every step runs in Python in the TFHE deep dive.
  • CKKS: approximate bootstrapping. The linear part is almost free. A ciphertext at the last level (modulus \(q_0\)) is reinterpreted modulo a much larger Q (ModRaise), so it encrypts \(\Delta m + e + q_0 \cdot I(X)\) for a small integer polynomial I. Removing \(q_0 \cdot I\) is the "rounding". Homomorphic multiplication acts slot by slot (P7, Packing N values), but \(q_0 \cdot I\) sits in the coefficients, so first a homomorphic DFT built from rotations (CoeffToSlot) moves the coefficients into slots. There EvalMod computes "\(x \bmod q_0\)". As a function of x that is a sawtooth, and near multiples of \(q_0\), where the inputs are, it is close to \(\tfrac{q_0}{2\pi} \sin(2\pi x / q_0)\). A polynomial can approximate that sine well, for example a Chebyshev approximation (a polynomial that stays close to the function on a whole interval, not just near one point). SlotToCoeff moves the result back. The bootstrapping key is the set of rotation and relinearization keys these steps need. See the CKKS deep dive §5.
  • BGV / BFV: digit extraction. Switch to a small modulus, compute the linear part homomorphically, then apply digit extraction, a polynomial that keeps each coefficient's message digits and clears the low digits holding the noise. It is exact but high-degree and slow, so most BGV/BFV deployments stay leveled (01 §3).

9. Programmable bootstrapping#

TFHE's test polynomial v is a lookup table: coefficient j holds the encoded message for the phase sector that j falls in. Plain rounding is the identity table. Store \(\Delta \cdot f(\text{sector of } j)\) instead, and the same bootstrap outputs \(\mathrm{Enc}(f(m))\): same keys, same cost, and f is a public table. This is programmable bootstrapping (PBS), a name due to Chillotti, Joye and Paillier (2021).

Why it is a superpower:

  • Non-linear functions are hard everywhere else. BGV, BFV and CKKS have only + and ×, so a comparison, sign, ReLU or max must be a polynomial. Good approximations need high degree, hence many levels, hence extra bootstraps (05 §4). Some functions are not polynomials at all: on \(\mathbb{Z}_4\), \([m \ge 2]\) cannot be built from + and × mod 4, because \(f(2) - f(0) \equiv 2f'(0) \pmod 4\) is even for every integer polynomial f, while \([2 \ge 2] - [0 \ge 2] = 1\) is odd.
  • In TFHE they ride along for free. TFHE bootstraps every few operations anyway. Demo 4 computes \(m^2 \bmod 4\), \([m \ge 2]\) and \(3 - m\) with the same keys, resetting the noise each time.
  • Several inputs fit in one table. Pack two 2-bit numbers into one 4-bit message \(4x + y\) (a linear operation) and index the table by both: demo 4 computes \([x < y]\) and \(\max(x, y)\) with one bootstrap each.

The limits: messages must be small (each extra bit needs a larger N), so 32- or 64-bit integers are split into blocks and cost many bootstraps per operation, and each bootstrap is a sequential chain of n steps. Since 2024, "functional" CKKS bootstrapping has brought lookup tables to thousands of CKKS values at once (07 §1).

10. What bootstrapping costs#

Orders of magnitude only, from the sources in chapter 07 and the deep dives; several are vendor figures.

Refreshes Rough cost
Gentry–Halevi 2011 (first implementation) one bit 30 s to 30 min per bootstrap; public keys of 70 MB to 2.3 GB
TFHE / FHEW today one small integer, after almost every operation a few ms per CPU core; under 1 ms on an NVIDIA H100 (vendor figure); keys of tens of MB
CKKS today up to \(2^{15}\) values, when the levels run out seconds on a multi-core CPU, tens to hundreds of ms on a GPU; uses roughly 10–18 levels; 20–30 bits of precision afterwards
BGV / BFV packed integers, rarely minutes in HElib (Halevi–Shoup: under 3 min for 1024 packed values, one core, about 80-bit security)

The toy's bootstrap took about 170 ms on the machine used for this chapter, the time of roughly 150 000 to 170 000 toy additions (demo 5; timings vary between runs and machines). That ratio belongs to a pure-Python toy, but the lesson holds everywhere: a bootstrap costs orders of magnitude more than an addition or a multiplication. Hence compilers that place bootstraps carefully, deployments that stay leveled when the circuit is known and shallow, and research aimed squarely at bootstrapping: CRYPTO 2026 work cuts the sequential NTT depth of FHEW/TFHE bootstrapping from 500+ to 3 under a new assumption, and accelerators such as Intel's Heracles target these pipelines (07).

12. How this connects to homomorphic encryption#

This chapter closes the primer's story. One-time-pad keys add (P1); AES scrambles all algebra away (P2, P3); RSA, ElGamal and Paillier keep one operation (P4, P6); security means IND-CPA, and HE accepts malleability on purpose (P3, P5); LWE's noise brings security and limits computation (P7); multiplication works but costs size and noise, which relinearization and modulus switching manage for a fixed number of levels (P8). Bootstrapping removes the last limit: by evaluating its own decryption on an encrypted key, a scheme resets its noise whenever it needs to, and leveled HE becomes fully homomorphic.

It also completes compactness (01 §2): a leveled scheme's parameters still depend on the depth of f, while with bootstrapping one parameter set works for every f. The price is time, and every deployment makes that trade: leveled when the circuit is known and shallow, bootstrapped when it is deep or unbounded. The lifecycle in 03 §9 shows where the bootstrap sits.

13. Check yourself#

  1. A server holds a ciphertext with 1 bit of noise budget left. Why can't it decrypt and re-encrypt, and what does it do instead?
  2. In demo 2 the step-11 ciphertext had noise \(2^{27.2}\) and its bootstrapped version \(2^{17.6}\). Where did the \(2^{27.2}\) go, and where did the \(2^{17.6}\) come from?
  3. Bootstrapping a fresh ciphertext raised its noise from \(2^{9.8}\) to \(2^{16.9}\). Is bootstrapping broken? When should a server bootstrap?
  4. A scheme handles multiplicative depth 12 before its noise overflows. Its decryption circuit has depth (a) 11, (b) 12, (c) 13. When is it bootstrappable, and what helps otherwise?
  5. Gentry's chain of keys needs no circular-security assumption. Why don't libraries use it? Do TFHE's two keys s and S avoid the assumption?
  6. In the toy, the identity table and the \([m \ge 2]\) table cost exactly the same. Why? And why could a scheme with only + and × mod 4 never compute \([m \ge 2]\) on \(\mathbb{Z}_4\)?

Answers#

  1. Decryption needs s, which the server must never have. It evaluates the circuit \(D_c\), with the ciphertext's numbers as constants, homomorphically on the bootstrapping key and gets \(\mathrm{Enc}(D_c(s)) = \mathrm{Enc}(m)\) with reset noise.
  2. The \(2^{27.2}\) was part of the value \(\varphi = \Delta m + e\) computed under encryption, and the homomorphic rounding removed it, as ordinary decryption would. The old ciphertext entered only as noise-free constants. The \(2^{17.6}\) is the noise of the bootstrapping and key-switching keys, grown by the bootstrap's own operations: a level fixed by the parameters.
  3. It is not broken: bootstrapping resets the noise to a fixed level, which may be above a fresh encryption's, and what matters is the room left (6–8 ×3 steps in the toy). Bootstrap when the public noise bound nears the limit, never after it: a ciphertext that already decrypts wrongly comes out as a clean encryption of the wrong value.
  4. (a) Yes: 11 + 1 ≤ 12, so a refresh plus one gate fits, and repeating "gate + refresh" evaluates any circuit. (b) A refresh fits but no gate after it, so no progress: not bootstrappable. (c) Not even a refresh fits. Make decryption shallower (squashing) or pick larger parameters so that L grows.
  5. A chain needs one key per level, key generation that grows with the depth, and a depth fixed in advance: a leveled scheme again. One self-encrypted key gives constant-size keys and unbounded depth for the price of an assumption with no known attack. TFHE's keys do not avoid it: s is encrypted under S and S under s, a cycle of length 2.
  6. The table is just the public coefficients of the test polynomial; blind rotation, sample extraction and key switching are the same whatever it holds. With only + and × mod 4 you compute polynomials, and for those \(f(2) - f(0) \equiv 2f'(0) \pmod 4\) is even, while \([2 \ge 2] - [0 \ge 2] = 1\) is odd.

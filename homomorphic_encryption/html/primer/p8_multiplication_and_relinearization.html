P8 · Multiplying ciphertexts: relinearization and modulus switching#

← P7 Lattices and LWE · index · next: P9 Bootstrapping →

P7 ended with an encryption scheme that can add: pads add, messages add, noises add. This chapter makes it multiply. Multiplication works, but it has two side effects that shape all of practical homomorphic encryption: the ciphertext gets bigger, and the noise gets much bigger. The fixes for those two side effects are the words that make chapter 01 hard to read: relinearization, key switching, modulus switching, levels and multiplicative depth.

In this chapter

  • Multiply two LWE ciphertexts by hand and see why the product has three parts and decrypts with the key vector (1, s, s²).
  • Say what "relinearization" literally means, build a relinearization key, and see why the naive version destroys the message while digit (gadget) decomposition saves it.
  • Recognise key switching as the general tool behind relinearization and slot rotations, and know why it dominates the cost of FHE.
  • Use modulus switching to shrink the noise, and explain why that helps even though the noise-to-modulus ratio stays the same.
  • Count levels and multiplicative depth, and see why FHE programmers prefer trees to chains.
  • Tell evaluation keys apart from the secret key.

Companion script: code/primer/p8_relinearization.py. Run it from the homomorphic_encryption/ folder with python3 code/primer/p8_relinearization.py. Its output is split into numbered parts, and the text says which part shows what.

Chapter 03 §4–6 is the compact version of this chapter. Read it afterwards as a summary.

1. The starting point: ciphertexts as (c0, c1)#

In P7 an LWE ciphertext is a pair (a, b) with

\[b = \langle a, s \rangle + \Delta m + e \pmod q, \qquad \Delta = \lfloor q/t \rfloor\]

To decrypt, you subtract the pad and round. The phase is \(\varphi = b - \langle a, s\rangle = \Delta m + e\), and the message is \(\mathrm{round}(\varphi/\Delta) \bmod t\). This is correct while \(\lvert e\rvert < \Delta/2\). In the ring version at the end of P7, a, s, e and m are polynomials in \(R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[X]/(X^N+1)\), and \(\langle a, s\rangle\) becomes the product \(a \cdot s\).

From now on we write the same ciphertext differently, as chapter 03 does:

\[\mathsf{ct} = (c_0, c_1) = (b, -a) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad c_0 + c_1 \cdot s = b - a \cdot s = \Delta m + e \pmod q\]

This is the same thing written differently. The numbers are the same; a minus sign has moved. The new form is convenient because decryption is now a dot product of the ciphertext with the key vector (1, s):

\[\langle \mathsf{ct}, (1, s) \rangle = c_0 \cdot 1 + c_1 \cdot s = \Delta m + e \pmod q\]

Every operation in this chapter is built to keep this one invariant true: ciphertext · key vector = Δ · message + something small. Addition keeps it, because \((c_0 + c_0') + (c_1 + c_1')s = \Delta(m_1 + m_2) + (e_1 + e_2)\).

The running example#

To compute by hand, we shrink polynomials to single numbers (N = 1). This is hopelessly insecure, since you could find s by trying ten values. The arithmetic, though, is exactly what the real scheme does.

Parameter Value
ciphertext modulus q 10000
plaintext modulus t 10
scaling factor Δ = q/t 1000 (decryption is correct while the noise stays below 500)
secret key s 3

Two ciphertexts, made as in P7: pick a random a, set b = a·s + Δm + e mod q, and write (b, −a). Numbers mod q are stored centred, between −5000 and 4999. Small representatives keep later products small.

a m e c0 = b c1 = −a c0 + c1·s mod q decrypts to
ct1 4321 2 2 12963 + 2002 = 14965 → 4965 −4321 4965 − 12963 = −7998 ≡ 2002 round(2.002) = 2
ct2 2718 3 −1 8154 + 2999 = 11153 → 1153 −2718 1153 − 8154 = −7001 ≡ 2999 round(2.999) = 3

Measuring noise#

We need a number for "how noisy is this ciphertext". We use this one throughout:

  • noise = the largest coefficient, in absolute value, of \(c_0 + c_1 s - \Delta m\), reduced mod q into the centred range. For single numbers it is just \(\lvert \varphi - \Delta m\rvert\). ct1 has noise 2, ct2 has noise 1.
  • Decryption is guaranteed correct while noise < Δ/2.
  • noise budget = \(\log_2\big((\Delta/2)/\text{noise}\big)\) bits: how many more bits the noise can grow before decryption breaks. It is essentially the "invariant noise budget" that libraries such as Microsoft SEAL report. A fresh ciphertext of the script's toy BFV (q = 2^60, Δ = 2^56) has noise about 5 and a budget of about 52.7 bits (part 2 of the script).

2. Multiplying two ciphertexts: the tensor product#

We want a ciphertext of \(m_1 m_2\). The server only has the ciphertexts. But both of them satisfy a decryption equation, so we multiply the two equations.

Multiply the left-hand sides and expand:

\[(c_0 + c_1 s)(c_0' + c_1' s) = c_0 c_0' + c_0 c_1' s + c_1 c_0' s + c_1 c_1' s^2\]

Group by powers of s:

\[= \underbrace{c_0 c_0'}_{d_0} + \underbrace{(c_0 c_1' + c_1 c_0')}_{d_1}\, s + \underbrace{c_1 c_1'}_{d_2}\, s^2\]

Multiply the right-hand sides:

\[(\Delta m_1 + e_1)(\Delta m_2 + e_2) = \Delta^2 m_1 m_2 + \Delta (m_1 e_2 + m_2 e_1) + e_1 e_2\]

Put together:

\[d_0 + d_1 s + d_2 s^2 = \Delta^2 m_1 m_2 + \Delta (m_1 e_2 + m_2 e_1) + e_1 e_2 \pmod q\]

The key observation: \(d_0, d_1, d_2\) are products of ciphertext numbers only. The server can compute them without knowing s. The triple (d0, d1, d2) is a ciphertext of \(m_1 m_2\) whose key vector is (1, s, s²). It is called the tensor product of the two ciphertexts: it consists of all pairwise products \(c_i c_j'\), with the two mixed ones added together.

By hand, with the running example:

d0 = 4965 · 1153                       =   5 724 645
d1 = 4965 · (−2718) + (−4321) · 1153   = −18 476 983
d2 = (−4321) · (−2718)                 =  11 744 478
check: d0 + 3·d1 + 9·d2 = 55 993 998 = (−7998) · (−7001)   (product of the two phases)

Three problems are visible already. The message sits at the wrong scale (Δ² instead of Δ), the noise term has changed shape, and the ciphertext has grown to three parts. The next two sections take them in turn.

3. The scale problem and the noise problem#

3.1 The message is at the wrong scale#

In the running example Δ² = 1 000 000, and mod q = 10000 that is 0. The message has vanished from the phase entirely. The toy BFV has the same problem: Δ² = 2¹¹² is a multiple of q = 2^60, and decrypting the raw tensor gives garbage (part 2 of the script).

BFV's fix is to multiply every component by \(t/q\) (which is \(1/\Delta\) here) and round to the nearest integer. That turns \(\Delta^2 m_1 m_2\) into \(\Delta m_1 m_2\), turns \(\Delta(m_1 e_2 + m_2 e_1)\) into \(m_1 e_2 + m_2 e_1\), and makes \(e_1 e_2\) negligible.

d · t/q rounded mod q, centred
d0 5724.645 5725 −4275
d1 −18476.983 −18477 1523
d2 11744.478 11744 1744

Decrypt with (1, s, s²) = (1, 3, 9): −4275 + 3·1523 + 9·1744 = 15990 ≡ 5990, and round(5990/1000) = 6 = 2·3. The noise is 5990 − 6000 = −10.

Why the scaling is done over the integers (optional on a first read). Mod q you cannot divide by Δ at all: Δ divides q (q = t·Δ), so Δ has no inverse mod q. Nor can you reduce mod q first and scale afterwards, because the two steps do not commute: a wrap-around by q before the scaling becomes a jump by t after it. So BFV computes d0, d1, d2 from the centred values as ordinary integers and scales before reducing. Over the integers each phase is \(\Delta m + e + q j\), where the integer j counts wrap-arounds. Here −7998 = 2002 − 10000, so \(j_1 = -1\), and likewise \(j_2 = -1\). When you multiply out \(\tfrac{t}{q}(\Delta m_1 + e_1 + q j_1)(\Delta m_2 + e_2 + q j_2)\), most q-terms become multiples of q and vanish, but \(t(j_1 e_2 + j_2 e_1)\) survives:

\[\text{new noise} \approx m_1 e_2 + m_2 e_1 + t\,(j_1 e_2 + j_2 e_1) + \text{rounding}\]

By hand: 2·(−1) + 3·2 + 10·((−1)(−1) + (−1)·2) = −6. The roundings in the table add 0.355 − 3·0.017 − 9·0.478 ≈ −4. Total −10, as measured.

3.2 Noise grows multiplicatively#

The dominant term is \(t\,(j_1 e_2 + j_2 e_1)\): the old noise gets multiplied by roughly t · j. That is where chapter 03's rule of thumb, "BFV multiplies the noise by about t · N", comes from. With polynomials, \(c_0 + c_1 s\) wraps around much more often: each coefficient of \(c_1 s\) is a sum of N products of a number up to q/2 with a small key coefficient, so the wrap count j becomes a polynomial with coefficients of order \(\sqrt N\) (up to about N in the worst case). The product \(j \cdot e\) is again a ring product, a sum of N terms, which adds roughly another factor \(\sqrt N\). Together the noise grows by about t · N. In the toy (N = 16, t = 16, so t · N = 256) one multiplication takes the noise from 5 to 540 (script, part 2), a factor of 108. That costs about 7 bits of budget, where an addition costs at most 1 bit.

BGV, the other exact scheme, puts the message in the low bits: \(c_0 + c_1 s = m + t e\). With t = 10, m = 7 and e = 3 the phase is 7 + 10·3 = 37. Decryption takes the phase in the centred range and reduces it mod t: 37 mod 10 = 7 (with e = −3 the phase is −23, and −23 mod 10 = 7 too). This works while \(\lvert m + t e \rvert < q/2\). BGV has no scale problem, but the product of two phases is \(m_1 m_2 + t\,(m_1 e_2 + m_2 e_1 + t\, e_1 e_2)\): the noise contains \(e_1 e_2\) and roughly squares. §8 shows how modulus switching tames that.

4. The size problem: ciphertexts grow#

The product has three components. Multiply it again and it grows further. A ciphertext with \(\alpha + 1\) components times one with \(\beta + 1\) components gives \(\alpha + \beta + 1\) components, with key vector \((1, s, \dots, s^{\alpha+\beta})\): you are multiplying two polynomials in s of degrees \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\). Part 3 of the script does exactly this and everything still decrypts:

x = Enc(m1)    2 components (0.23 KiB)  decrypts with (1, s, ..., s^1): True   budget 52.7 bits
x*y            3 components (0.35 KiB)  decrypts with (1, s, ..., s^2): True   budget 45.9 bits
(x*y) * z      4 components (0.47 KiB)  decrypts with (1, s, ..., s^3): True   budget 40.0 bits
(x*y) * (x*y)  5 components (0.59 KiB)  decrypts with (1, s, ..., s^4): True   budget 37.3 bits

Correct, but unacceptable. The ciphertext size now depends on how many multiplications made it, which breaks compactness (01 §2, P6). Multiplications also get slower: multiplying an \((\alpha + 1)\)-component ciphertext by a \((\beta + 1)\)-component one takes \((\alpha + 1)(\beta + 1)\) ring products. We want to return to two components after every multiplication.

5. Relinearization: making decryption linear in s again#

5.1 What the word means#

The decryption expression of (d0, d1, d2) is \(d_0 + d_1 s + d_2 s^2\): a quadratic polynomial in the unknown s. The decryption expression of a normal ciphertext, \(c_0 + c_1 s\), is linear in s. Relinearization means exactly this: turn the quadratic expression back into a linear one, i.e. convert (d0, d1, d2) into a pair (c0, c1) that decrypts to the same message with (1, s). Brakerski and Vaikuntanathan introduced the technique and the name in 2011. In their plain-LWE version the quadratic terms are products \(s_i s_j\) of secret coordinates; in the ring version it is the single term \(s^2\).

Only \(d_2 s^2\) is a problem. Suppose the server could find a pair (u0, u1) with \(u_0 + u_1 s \approx d_2 s^2\). Then

\[(d_0 + u_0) + (d_1 + u_1)\, s = d_0 + d_1 s + (u_0 + u_1 s) \approx d_0 + d_1 s + d_2 s^2\]

so (d0 + u0, d1 + u1) is a two-component ciphertext of the same message. The server cannot compute \(d_2 s^2\) itself, because that needs s.

5.2 The relinearization key: an encryption of s squared#

The key owner, who knows s, publishes a relinearization key: an encryption of \(s^2\) under s, in the same (c0, c1) form as any ciphertext.

\[\mathrm{rlk} = (k_0, k_1) = (-a s + \varepsilon + s^2,\ a), \qquad k_0 + k_1 s = s^2 + \varepsilon\]

Here a is random and ε is a small error, as in any LWE encryption. The second component is +a where §1 had −a: a is uniformly random, so its sign does not matter, and keys are usually written with +a (chapter 03 does too). By hand, with a = 1234 and ε = 2: \(k_0 = -3702 + 2 + 9 = -3691 \equiv 6309\). Check: 6309 + 3·1234 = 10011 ≡ 11 = 9 + 2.

5.3 The naive way, and why it explodes#

The obvious move is to multiply the key by \(d_2\): \((u_0, u_1) = (d_2 k_0, d_2 k_1)\). Then

\[u_0 + u_1 s = d_2 (k_0 + k_1 s) = d_2 s^2 + d_2 \varepsilon\]

The term \(d_2 \varepsilon\) is the problem. ε is small, but \(d_2\) is a full-size number mod q, as random-looking as anything mod q. Their product is huge.

By hand, \(d_2 = 1744\): u = (1744·6309, 1744·1234) mod q = (2896, 2096). The new ciphertext is (−4275 + 2896, 1523 + 2096) = (−1379, 3619). Its phase is −1379 + 3·3619 = 9478, which decrypts to 9 instead of 6. The added noise is 1744·2 = 3488, far above the limit of 500. In the toy BFV the result is the same, at scale: the noise reaches \(2^{59}\) against a limit of \(2^{55}\) (part 4 of the script).

5.4 Why the key can be public#

The relinearization key goes to the server, so it must be safe to publish.

  • It looks random. (−a s + ε, a) is an RLWE sample, and under the RLWE assumption (P7) it is indistinguishable from random. Adding \(s^2\) to its first part is like adding a message to a one-time pad: the pad hides it.
  • It does not let anyone decrypt. Decryption needs \(c_0 + c_1 s\), so it needs s. The key only converts "something times \(s^2\)" into "something linear in s", and the result is still encrypted under s. It is an evaluation key: material that lets the server compute, not decrypt (§10).
  • The caveat: circular security. The key encrypts a function of the secret key under that same key, a case the RLWE assumption does not cover. Assuming it is still safe is a mild form of circular security; Brakerski–Vaikuntanathan and BGV avoided it in theory with a fresh key per level, while libraries use one key, and no attack is known. Bootstrapping needs the strong form, explained in P9 §7.

6. Digit (gadget) decomposition: small multipliers, small noise#

6.1 The idea#

The added noise is (multiplier) × (key error). The error cannot be zero, because without it the key is insecure. So make the multiplier small. Write \(d_2\) in base w:

\[d_2 = \sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1} d_2^{(i)}\, w^i, \qquad 0 \le d_2^{(i)} < w, \qquad \ell = \lceil \log_w q \rceil\]

The key owner publishes ℓ keys, one for each power of w:

\[\mathrm{rlk}_i = (k_{0,i},\ k_{1,i}) = (-a_i s + \varepsilon_i + w^i s^2,\ a_i), \qquad k_{0,i} + k_{1,i}\, s = w^i s^2 + \varepsilon_i\]

The server multiplies each key by its digit and adds:

\[u_0 = \sum_i d_2^{(i)} k_{0,i}, \qquad u_1 = \sum_i d_2^{(i)} k_{1,i}\]
\[u_0 + u_1 s = \sum_i d_2^{(i)} \big(w^i s^2 + \varepsilon_i\big) = d_2\, s^2 + \sum_i d_2^{(i)} \varepsilon_i\]

The added noise is at most \(\ell \cdot (w-1) \cdot \max\lvert\varepsilon_i\rvert\) (for polynomials, times N), instead of \(d_2 \varepsilon\), which is of order q · ε. It is the schoolbook trick for multiplying by a long number: pre-multiply by each power of the base, then only ever multiply by single digits.

6.2 By hand, in base 10#

Same example: q = 10000, s = 3, so \(s^2 = 9\). Take w = 10, so ℓ = 4 keys:

i \(w^i s^2\) \(a_i\) \(\varepsilon_i\) \(k_{0,i} = -3a_i + \varepsilon_i + w^i s^2\) mod q check \(k_{0,i} + 3a_i\) mod q
0 9 1234 2 6309 11 = 9 + 2
1 90 5678 −1 3055 89 = 90 − 1
2 900 9012 1 3865 901 = 900 + 1
3 9000 3456 3 8635 9003 = 9000 + 3

Row 0 is the naive key from §5.2. The digits of \(d_2 = 1744\), least significant first, are (4, 4, 7, 1), since 1744 = 4 + 4·10 + 7·100 + 1·1000. Then:

u0 = 4·6309 + 4·3055 + 7·3865 + 1·8635 = 73146 ≡ 3146
u1 = 4·1234 + 4·5678 + 7·9012 + 1·3456 = 94188 ≡ 4188
check: u0 + 3·u1 = 15710 ≡ 5710,   d2·s² = 15696 ≡ 5696,   difference 14 = 4·2 + 4·(−1) + 7·1 + 1·3
relinearized ciphertext: (−4275 + 3146, 1523 + 4188) = (−1129, 5711) ≡ (−1129, −4289)
decrypt: −1129 + 3·(−4289) = −13996 ≡ 6004  →  round(6.004) = 6

The relinearization added 14 to the noise (from −10 to 4), where the naive key added 3488. The bound is 4 · 9 · 3 = 108.

6.3 The trade-off: key size against noise#

A smaller base w means more digits. That gives a bigger key and more ring multiplications per relinearization, but less noise. Part 5 of the script relinearizes the same toy product ciphertext with different bases (its noise before relinearization is \(2^{9.1}\)):

  base w  digits   key size  noise after  budget left
     2^4      15   3.52 KiB       2^10.1    44.9 bits
    2^10       6   1.41 KiB       2^15.3    39.7 bits
    2^15       4   0.94 KiB       2^19.7    35.3 bits
    2^20       3   0.70 KiB       2^23.8    31.2 bits
    2^30       2   0.47 KiB       2^34.4    20.6 bits
    2^60       1   0.23 KiB       2^58.9    -3.9 bits

The last row, w = q, is the naive key: one digit, which is \(d_2\) itself. The added noise grows roughly in proportion to w (here between about 15·w and 40·w), while the key size grows with the number of digits \(\ell = \lceil \log_w q \rceil\).

Names. Brakerski–Vaikuntanathan decompose into bits (base 2), and BGV call the two halves BitDecomp (split into bits) and Powersof2 (multiply the key by 1, 2, 4, …). Today the vector \(g = (1, w, w^2, \dots)\) is called the gadget, and splitting into digits is gadget decomposition.

The other fix: a special modulus. Fan and Vercauteren (2012) gave two relinearization variants: one with digit decomposition as here, and one that uses a larger auxiliary modulus instead. The second publishes an encryption of \(P \cdot s^2\) modulo \(P q\), for a large "special" modulus P, so that \(k_0 + k_1 s = P s^2 + \varepsilon \pmod{Pq}\). The server multiplies the key by the whole \(d_2\), which gives \(P\, d_2 s^2 + d_2 \varepsilon\), then divides by P and rounds: a modulus switch from Pq back to q (§8). The result encrypts \(d_2 s^2\) with extra noise \(d_2 \varepsilon / P\) plus a small rounding term. The harmful product \(d_2 \varepsilon\), of order \(q \varepsilon\), has been divided by P, so it is small when P is about as large as q. By hand with P = 10000: \(1744 \cdot 2 / 10000 \approx 0.35\), and the relinearized ciphertext decrypts to 6 with its noise still −10. The price: the key lives modulo Pq, and a bigger modulus costs security (P7 §4), so \(\log_2(Pq)\) must fit the table of 02 §6. Modern libraries combine both ideas in hybrid key switching: split \(d_2\) into a few large digits, aligned with the RNS primes of §9.1, and pick P about the size of one digit. Then (digit × ε) / P stays small with a much smaller P (03 §5).

7. Key switching: the general tool#

Relinearization takes a term \(d_2 \cdot s^2\), a piece of ciphertext "under the key \(s^2\)", and re-expresses it under the key s. Nothing in §6 used the fact that \(s^2\) is a square. Replace it by any other key s' and you get key switching.

  • Input: a ciphertext with \(c_0 + c_1 s' = \Delta m + e\), i.e. encrypted under s'.
  • Key-switching key: encryptions of \(w^i s'\) under s, i.e. \((k_{0,i}, k_{1,i})\) with \(k_{0,i} + k_{1,i} s = w^i s' + \varepsilon_i\).
  • Output: decompose \(c_1\) into digits \(c_1^{(i)}\), compute \(u_j = \sum_i c_1^{(i)} k_{j,i}\), and return \((c_0 + u_0,\ u_1)\). Then \((c_0 + u_0) + u_1 s = \Delta m + e + \sum_i c_1^{(i)} \varepsilon_i\): the same message, now under s.

BGV (2012) introduced this general view and the name. Its uses:

  • Relinearization: s' = s².
  • Rotations. Batching packs many values into the slots of one plaintext (P7). The map \(X \mapsto X^k\) for odd k (a Galois automorphism), applied to both components of a ciphertext, permutes the slots, for example rotating them by one position. It also turns the key s(X) into s(X^k), written \(\sigma_k(s)\) or \(\sigma(s)\) in the main notes. A key switch with a Galois key (encryptions of \(w^i s(X^k)\) under s) brings it back. Each rotation amount needs its own key, or is composed from power-of-two rotations (05 §2).
  • Moving to a different key in general: to a smaller key or dimension, for example at the end of TFHE bootstrapping (03 §8), or between schemes (03 §5).

Cost. One key switch needs about 2ℓ ring multiplications (each an NTT-based product, P7) plus the decomposition. A key-switching key is ℓ ciphertexts. With chapter 02's numbers (N = 2^14, log q = 438), one ciphertext is about 1.8 MB (02 §6). A key with ℓ = 3 digits is then about 5.4 MB, and a set of 30 rotation keys about 160 MB. Key switching is the most expensive routine in RLWE-based FHE and the main target of hardware acceleration.

8. Modulus switching: dividing the noise away#

8.1 The operation#

Take an LWE ciphertext mod q and scale every number down to a smaller modulus q', rounding to the nearest integer. This section goes back to P7's (a, b) form with a vector mask a, because the rounding acts on each coordinate of a separately; \((c_0, c_1) = (b, -a)\) works the same way.

\[a' = \Big\lfloor \tfrac{q'}{q}\, a \Big\rceil, \qquad b' = \Big\lfloor \tfrac{q'}{q}\, b \Big\rceil \pmod{q'}\]

The rounding of a is done coordinate by coordinate. Why does the message survive? Over the integers, \(b - \langle a, s\rangle = \Delta m + e + j q\) for some integer j. Multiply by \(q'/q\), with q and q' both multiples of t and \(\Delta' = q'/t\): \(\tfrac{q'}{q} b - \langle \tfrac{q'}{q} a, s\rangle = \Delta' m + \tfrac{q'}{q} e + j q'\). Rounding changes each number by at most 1/2: \(b' = \tfrac{q'}{q} b + r_b\) and \(a' = \tfrac{q'}{q} a + r_a\). So

\[b' - \langle a', s \rangle = \Delta' m + \underbrace{\tfrac{q'}{q}\, e + r_b - \langle r_a, s\rangle}_{e'} + j q'\]
\[\lvert e' \rvert \le \tfrac{q'}{q} \lvert e \rvert + \tfrac{1}{2}\big(1 + \lVert s \rVert_1\big)\]

The noise shrinks by the same factor as the modulus, plus a rounding term. The rounding term is small because the secret is small: one more reason secrets are ternary. Lemma 1 of the BGV paper states the same fact for messages in the low bits (there the rounding must also keep the ciphertext unchanged mod 2, and the rounding term is at most \(\lVert s \rVert_1\)). It needs no key at all; anyone can do it.

Take q = 10000 → q' = 100, i.e. divide by 100. Then t = 10, Δ = 1000 → Δ' = 10, s = (1, −1, 1), m = 7, and a noise of e = 300 left over from earlier work. With a = (4821, 1377, 6254): ⟨a, s⟩ = 4821 − 1377 + 6254 = 9698, and b = 9698 + 7000 + 300 = 16998 ≡ 6998.

8.3 Why shrink the noise if the ratio stays the same?#

The BGV paper asks exactly this. The answer: multiplication cares about the absolute noise. Take BGV-style noise that squares at every multiplication. In an idealized model that ignores the factors t and N, start with \(q = 2^{100}\) and fresh noise \(2^{10}\). Decryption fails once the noise reaches q.

no switching: noise with switching: modulus, noise
fresh \(2^{10}\) \(2^{100}\), \(2^{10}\)
after multiplication 1 \(2^{20}\) \(2^{100}\), \(2^{20}\) → switch to \(2^{90}\), \(2^{10}\)
after multiplication 2 \(2^{40}\) \(2^{90}\), \(2^{20}\) → switch to \(2^{80}\), \(2^{10}\)
after multiplication 3 \(2^{80}\) \(2^{80}\), \(2^{20}\) → switch to \(2^{70}\), \(2^{10}\)
after multiplication 4 \(2^{160}\): fails \(2^{70}\), \(2^{20}\) → switch to \(2^{60}\), \(2^{10}\)
… every multiplication costs 10 bits of modulus
after multiplication 8 \(2^{30}\), \(2^{20}\) → switch to \(2^{20}\), \(2^{10}\); a 9th would fail

Three multiplications without switching, eight with it. In general, with fresh noise B and q ≈ B^L you get about log₂ L multiplications without switching and about L with it: exponentially more. The noise stays near its fresh size; the modulus is what gets used up.

8.4 BGV and BFV#

In BGV the message sits in the low bits, so plain rounding would disturb it; BGV rounds to the nearest values that keep the ciphertext unchanged mod t (03 §6). With the message in the high bits, as here, plain rounding is enough. In BFV, the t/q step of every multiplication (§3) already divides the noise. That is why Brakerski's 2012 paper is titled "FHE without modulus switching", and why BFV noise grows by a roughly fixed factor per multiplication instead of squaring. BFV implementations still offer modulus switching, mainly to shrink a result ciphertext before sending it back.

9. Levels, leveled HE and multiplicative depth#

9.1 Levels and leveled HE#

In BGV and CKKS, the parameters fix a modulus chain \(q_L > q_{L-1} > \dots > q_0\). A fresh ciphertext starts at the top, level L. Each multiplication is followed by one switch down, which uses up one level. In real libraries the modulus is a product of primes of 30–60 bits, \(q_\ell = p_0 p_1 \cdots p_\ell\), and each number mod \(q_\ell\) is stored as its residues mod each prime. This residue number system (RNS) is the Chinese Remainder Theorem put to work: all arithmetic runs on machine words, one prime at a time (02 §5, which writes the primes \(q_i\)). Dividing by the last prime \(p_\ell\) and dropping it is a modulus switch from \(q_\ell\) to \(q_{\ell-1}\), so going down a level literally means dropping one prime. At level 0 there is no room for another multiplication: decrypt, or bootstrap (P9). To combine two ciphertexts at different levels, first switch the higher one down. BFV has no explicit chain by default, but its noise budget plays the same role: each multiplication spends a roughly fixed number of bits, and people still speak of "levels" for the number of multiplications that fit.

A leveled scheme is set up for a maximum depth L chosen in advance. It can then evaluate any circuit of depth at most L, of any size, without bootstrapping. The parameters depend on the depth, not on the number of operations. That is the "leveled FHE without bootstrapping" of BGV's title, and most deployed BGV, BFV and CKKS applications work this way (04).

9.2 Multiplicative depth: chains and trees#

Multiplicative depth is the largest number of multiplications on any path from an input to the output. P6 §7 draws the product of eight values both ways: as a chain of depth 7 and as a balanced tree of depth 3, each with seven multiplications. Depth, rather than the number of operations, decides how big q must be. In real BFV and CKKS each level costs very roughly 30–60 bits of q (the table in 02 §6 works out to about 45 bits per multiplication). q then decides how big N must be for security, and N decides the cost of everything.

Part 6 of the script squares a ciphertext until it breaks, then computes the eight-value product in both shapes:

x        fresh    budget  52.4 bits
x^2      depth 1  budget  35.2 bits   decrypts correctly: True
x^4      depth 2  budget  28.2 bits   decrypts correctly: True
x^8      depth 3  budget  21.1 bits   decrypts correctly: True
x^16     depth 4  budget  14.7 bits   decrypts correctly: True
x^32     depth 5  budget   8.2 bits   decrypts correctly: True
x^64     depth 6  budget   1.2 bits   decrypts correctly: True

x1*x2*...*x8, same 7 multiplications, two shapes:
chain ((((((x1*x2)*x3)*x4)*x5)*x6)*x7)*x8  depth 7  budget  -1.6 bits   correct: False
tree  ((x1*x2)*(x3*x4))*((x5*x6)*(x7*x8))  depth 3  budget  20.8 bits   correct: True

How to read it:

  • The first multiplication costs 17 bits. Most of that is the relinearization noise of §6 (about \(2^{19.6}\)), which is added once.
  • After that, every level costs about 7 bits, because the noise is multiplied by a roughly fixed factor (§3.2). The budget falls linearly with depth.
  • With the same seven multiplications, the chain runs out of budget and decrypts wrongly. The tree finishes with 20.8 bits to spare. FHE compilers rebalance expression trees for exactly this reason. Computing \(x^{16}\) by repeated squaring costs depth 4, not 15.

9.3 CKKS rescaling, in one paragraph#

CKKS (Cheon–Kim–Kim–Song, 2017) encrypts real or complex numbers scaled by a large Δ such as \(2^{40}\), and accepts the noise as a small rounding error. One ciphertext holds \(N/2\) complex values, not \(N\): the slots are the values of the message polynomial at the complex roots of \(X^N + 1\), and since the polynomial has real coefficients those values come in conjugate pairs, so only half of them are free. Decryption and decoding return \(z + e/\Delta\) for the encoded values \(z\): the noise is never rounded away, it becomes part of the result. That is why sharing a CKKS decryption can leak e, and with it the key (IND-CPA-D, P5 §8 and the IND-CPA-D deep dive).

After a multiplication the scale is Δ², exactly the problem of §3.1. Instead of BFV's t/q trick, CKKS rescales: it divides the ciphertext by the last prime of the chain, \(p_\ell \approx \Delta\) (the same name chapter 03 §7 uses), which is the modulus switch from \(q_\ell\) to \(q_{\ell-1} = q_\ell / p_\ell\) of §9.1. That brings the scale back to about Δ, divides the noise by \(p_\ell\) as well, and uses up one level. It is the fixed-point arithmetic habit of shifting right after each multiplication, done on ciphertexts. In CKKS, the modulus chain is literally a budget of multiplications. See deep_dives/ckks.md §4.

10. Evaluation keys versus the secret key#

Key What it is Who holds it What it allows
secret key s a small polynomial the client only decryption
public key an encryption of 0 under s (P7) anyone encryption
relinearization key encryptions of \(w^i s^2\) under s the server relinearization after multiplying
Galois (rotation) keys encryptions of \(w^i s(X^k)\) under s, one per rotation the server slot rotations
bootstrapping key an encryption of the secret key (P9) the server resetting the noise

The last three are evaluation keys. Only the secret-key holder can make them, because making them needs s. They are public, and they are large: for BGV, BFV and CKKS they often dominate memory and bandwidth, from hundreds of MB to several GB (TFHE's are tens of MB, P9 §10). None of them lets anyone decrypt, because each is an encryption under s and removing that encryption needs s. All of them encrypt values that depend on the key, so their security rests on RLWE plus a circular-security-style assumption (§5.4).

12. How this connects to homomorphic encryption#

P7 gave us encryption that can add. This chapter added multiplication, and with + and × we can evaluate any arithmetic circuit (P6). Multiplying ciphertexts works because the decryption equations multiply. It also makes ciphertexts bigger (the \(s^2\) term) and noisier (the noise is multiplied, not added). Relinearization, which is key switching with an evaluation key, fixes the size. Modulus switching, or BFV's built-in scaling, keeps the noise growth to a fixed number of bits per multiplication. What remains is a budget: L levels, spent one per level of multiplicative depth. That is a leveled HE scheme, and for circuits of known depth it is all you need. To compute without a depth limit, something must refill the budget. That is bootstrapping, in P9. For the compact version of this chapter, read 03 §4, §5 and §6.

13. Check yourself#

  1. Why does the product of two ciphertexts have three components, and why can the server compute them without the secret key?
    Show answer

    Multiplying the two decryption equations \((c_0 + c_1 s)(c_0' + c_1' s)\) gives terms in \(1\), \(s\) and \(s^2\), so three coefficients \(d_0, d_1, d_2\). Each is a product or a sum of products of ciphertext numbers. s only appears in the key vector (1, s, s²), which the server never needs.

  2. A ciphertext with 3 components is multiplied by one with 4 components, without relinearization. How many components does the result have, and which key vector decrypts it?
    Show answer

    3 + 4 − 1 = 6 components, decrypted with (1, s, s², s³, s⁴, s⁵): a degree-2 polynomial in s times a degree-3 one gives degree 5.

  3. Why does relinearizing with a single encryption of \(s^2\) fail? With w = 10, ℓ = 4 and key errors of absolute value at most 3, what is the largest noise that gadget relinearization can add for single numbers?
    Show answer

    The extra noise is \(d_2 \cdot \varepsilon\), and \(d_2\) is a full-size number mod q, so the product is huge (3488 against a limit of 500 in the example). With digits, the extra noise is \(\sum_i d_2^{(i)} \varepsilon_i\), at most 4 · 9 · 3 = 108.

  4. Modulus switching from q = 10000 to q' = 100 turned noise 300 into noise 3, but noise/Δ stayed 0.3. Why is that useful?
    Show answer

    The next multiplication acts on the absolute noise, not on the ratio. In BGV it roughly squares the noise: from B it would go to B², then B⁴, doubling its bit length each time. Switching down after each multiplication brings the noise back to about B, so every level costs a fixed log₂ B bits of modulus. That is the difference between about log₂ L and about L levels when q ≈ B^L (§8.3).

  5. What is the smallest multiplicative depth with which you can compute \(x^8 \cdot y\)? And \(x_1 x_2 \cdots x_{16}\)?
    Show answer

    \(x^8 y\) is a product of 9 factors, and a binary tree with 9 leaves has depth at least ⌈log₂ 9⌉ = 4. Depth 4 is achievable: \(x^2, x^4, x^8\) by squaring (depth 3), then times y. \(x_1 \cdots x_{16}\) has depth ⌈log₂ 16⌉ = 4 as a balanced tree, against 15 as a chain.

  6. The server holds the relinearization key and the Galois keys. Why can it still not decrypt, and which extra assumption is involved?
    Show answer

    Every evaluation key is an encryption under s. Using it produces ciphertexts that are still under s, and stripping an encryption under s requires s. Under RLWE the keys look random. Because they encrypt functions of s itself, their safety also needs a (weak) circular-security assumption.

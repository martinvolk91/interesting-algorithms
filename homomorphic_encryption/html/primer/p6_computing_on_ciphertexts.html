P6 · Computing on ciphertexts: homomorphisms and circuits#

← P5 Security definitions · index · next: P7 Lattices and learning with errors (LWE) →

Homomorphic encryption rests on one property: an operation on ciphertexts acts on the hidden plaintexts. Several classic public-key schemes already have this property for one operation. This chapter works through them, shows what they are used for, and then explains why one operation is not enough. Any program can be written as a circuit of additions and multiplications, so a scheme needs both, as many times as the circuit is deep. Getting there took thirty years, and it needed the new mathematics of P7.

In this chapter

  • Say precisely what "homomorphic" means, and why the precise statement is about decryption.
  • Run Paillier and Goldwasser–Micali by hand and know which operation each one supports.
  • Turn a program (additions, comparisons, if, loops) into a circuit of exclusive-or (XOR) and AND gates, and count its gates and its multiplicative depth.
  • Define partially, somewhat, leveled and fully homomorphic encryption (PHE, SHE, LHE, FHE), and say where compactness comes in.
  • Explain why the group-based schemes of P4 stop at one operation, and why getting both took until 2009.

Companion script: code/primer/p6_partially_homomorphic.py. Run it from the homomorphic_encryption/ folder with python3 code/primer/p6_partially_homomorphic.py. Its output is split into numbered parts, and the text says which part shows what.

1. What homomorphic means#

"Homomorphic" is Greek for "same shape". A homomorphism is a map between two algebraic structures that respects their operations:

\[f(a \circ b) = f(a) * f(b)\]

You already know several:

  • \(e^{a+b} = e^a \cdot e^b\): the exponential turns addition into multiplication. Slide rules used the inverse map, the logarithm, to turn multiplication into addition.
  • Reduction mod \(n\) respects both operations: \((a + b) \bmod n\) and \((a \cdot b) \bmod n\) can be computed from \(a \bmod n\) and \(b \bmod n\). That is why modular arithmetic works at all. A map that respects two operations is a ring homomorphism.
  • Parity: the parity of \(a + b\) is the XOR of the parities of \(a\) and \(b\).

For encryption we want an operation \(\circledast\) on ciphertexts that matches an operation \(\circ\) on plaintexts. The precise statement is about decryption:

\[\mathrm{Dec}(c_1 \circledast c_2) = \mathrm{Dec}(c_1) \circ \mathrm{Dec}(c_2)\]

Why not write \(\mathrm{Enc}(m_1) \circledast \mathrm{Enc}(m_2) = \mathrm{Enc}(m_1 \circ m_2)\)? Because a secure scheme encrypts with fresh randomness (P5 shows that indistinguishability under chosen-plaintext attack, IND-CPA, forces this). Every message has many ciphertexts, so Enc is not a function. \(c_1 \circledast c_2\) is one of the ciphertexts that decrypt to \(m_1 \circ m_2\): in ElGamal or Paillier one that Enc could have produced with other randomness, in the lattice schemes of P7–P9 one with more noise than Enc would ever produce. The map that really is a homomorphism is Dec, from ciphertexts to plaintexts. Applied gate by gate through a whole computation \(f\), the same statement is HE's correctness rule (01 §2): whatever ciphertexts of \(m_1, \dots, m_k\) the server starts from, the result of evaluating \(f\) on them decrypts to \(f(m_1, \dots, m_k)\).

Two consequences are worth keeping:

  • The encryptions of the neutral element (0 for \(+\), 1 for \(\times\)) form the kernel of Dec. For the randomized public-key schemes of this chapter, security comes down to this: nobody without the key can tell whether a ciphertext lies in the kernel. Each scheme's hardness assumption is a statement of that kind. For ElGamal, telling an encryption of 1, \((g^y, h^y)\), from a random pair is exactly the decisional Diffie–Hellman (DDH) problem; for Paillier and Goldwasser–Micali (§3, §4) it is telling encryptions of 0 from random ciphertexts.
  • Homomorphic means malleable. Anyone, including an attacker, can turn a ciphertext into a related one, which rules out indistinguishability under adaptive chosen-ciphertext attack, IND-CCA2 (P5 §7). For HE it is the whole point.

2. Partially homomorphic schemes you have already met#

The one-time pad mod n: addition#

The pad of P1 is additively homomorphic (P1 §11 adds three ciphertexts mod 1000 by hand):

\[(m_1 + k_1) + (m_2 + k_2) = (m_1 + m_2) + (k_1 + k_2) \pmod n\]

The sum of two ciphertexts is an encryption of the sum under the key \(k_1 + k_2\). It is perfectly secure. The catch is the key: it is as long as the data, never reusable, and the key holder must track which keys were summed.

Multiplication fails, because \((m_1 + k_1)(m_2 + k_2)\) contains the cross terms \(m_1 k_2 + m_2 k_1\), which mix message and key. Keep that picture in mind: in P8 the same cross terms appear when two LWE ciphertexts are multiplied, and handling them is most of the work.

Textbook RSA: multiplication#

In textbook Rivest–Shamir–Adleman (RSA) encryption, \((m_1^e)(m_2^e) = (m_1 m_2)^e\), so multiplying ciphertexts multiplies plaintexts; P4 §4 checks \(\mathrm{Enc}(2) \cdot \mathrm{Enc}(3) \equiv \mathrm{Enc}(6) \pmod{3233}\) by hand. But textbook RSA is deterministic, so it is not IND-CPA secure, and the padding that fixes this, optimal asymmetric encryption padding (OAEP, P4 §4), destroys the homomorphism too.

ElGamal: multiplication, and exponential ElGamal: addition#

P4 §5 built both. ElGamal encrypts \(m\) as \((g^y, m \cdot h^y)\) with public key \(h = g^x\) and fresh randomness \(y\). The componentwise product of two ciphertexts is \((g^{y_1 + y_2}, m_1 m_2 \cdot h^{y_1 + y_2})\), an encryption of \(m_1 m_2\) with randomness \(y_1 + y_2\). Exponential ElGamal encrypts \(g^m\) instead of \(m\), so the same product gives \(g^{m_1 + m_2}\): addition. Decryption then returns \(g^m\), and recovering \(m\) is a discrete logarithm. That is only feasible when the result is small, such as a vote count.

3. Paillier: addition with a public key#

Pascal Paillier's 1999 scheme is the standard tool for adding under encryption. It is randomized, IND-CPA secure, and decrypts any result mod \(n\) directly, with no discrete logarithm.

Keys. \(n = pq\) for two large primes, \(g = n + 1\). The secret key is \(\lambda = \mathrm{lcm}(p-1, q-1)\) together with \(\mu = \lambda^{-1} \bmod n\). (This \(\lambda\) is Paillier's traditional name for the lcm; it has nothing to do with the security parameter λ of P5.) Ciphertexts live mod \(n^2\).

Encryption of \(m \in \mathbb{Z}_n\) with a random \(r\) coprime to \(n\):

\[\mathrm{Enc}(m; r) = (1 + n)^m \cdot r^n \bmod n^2\]

Why it works. Two facts:

  1. By the binomial theorem, \((1 + n)^m = 1 + m n + \binom{m}{2} n^2 + \dots \equiv 1 + m n \pmod{n^2}\). The message sits in plain view, linearly, in the "second digit" of the number written in base \(n\).
  2. The mask \(r^n\) is an \(n\)-th power, and raising it to \(\lambda\) removes it: \(r^{n\lambda} \equiv 1 \pmod{n^2}\), because the order of every element of \(\mathbb{Z}_{n^2}^*\) divides \(n\lambda\).

So \(c^\lambda \equiv (1 + n)^{m\lambda} \equiv 1 + (m\lambda \bmod n)\, n \pmod{n^2}\). Decryption reads off that digit and divides by \(\lambda\):

\[m = \frac{(c^\lambda \bmod n^2) - 1}{n} \cdot \mu \bmod n\]

Security. Telling \(n\)-th powers mod \(n^2\) (the encryptions of 0) from random elements is the decisional composite residuosity (DCR) problem, believed hard. Whoever factors \(n\) can compute \(\lambda\) and decrypt, so \(n\) must be at least as large as an RSA modulus (3072 bits for about 128-bit security), and a ciphertext has twice that size.

A hand-worked example#

Take \(p = 5\), \(q = 7\): \(n = 35\), \(n^2 = 1225\), \(g = 36\), \(\lambda = \mathrm{lcm}(4, 6) = 12\), \(\mu = 12^{-1} \bmod 35 = 3\) (since \(12 \cdot 3 = 36\)).

Step Computation Result
Encrypt 3 with \(r = 2\) \(36^3 \cdot 2^{35} = 106 \cdot 18 \bmod 1225\) 683
Encrypt 4 with \(r = 3\) \(36^4 \cdot 3^{35} = 141 \cdot 607 \bmod 1225\) 1062
Add \(683 \cdot 1062 \bmod 1225\) 146
Decrypt: raise to \(\lambda\) \(146^{12} \bmod 1225\) \(491 = 1 + 14 \cdot 35\)
Decrypt: read the digit, times \(\mu\) \(14 \cdot 3 \bmod 35\) 7

Note \(36^3 \bmod 1225 = 106 = 1 + 3 \cdot 35\): fact 1 at work.

What a Paillier server can do without the key#

Operation How Result decrypts to
Add two ciphertexts \(c_1 \cdot c_2 \bmod n^2\) \(m_1 + m_2\)
Multiply by a public constant \(k\) \(c^k \bmod n^2\) \(k \cdot m\)
Negate, subtract \(c^{n-1}\), then multiply \(-m\), \(m_1 - m_2\)
Add a public constant \(k\) \(c \cdot (1+n)^k\) \(m + k\)
Re-randomize \(c \cdot \mathrm{Enc}(0)\) \(m\), as a fresh-looking ciphertext

Multiplying by constants and adding gives every linear function with public coefficients: sums, averages (divide after decrypting), dot products with public weights \(\prod_i c_i^{w_i}\). In the example above, \(683^5 \bmod 1225 = 443\) decrypts to 15.

Re-randomization matters more than it looks. Without it, the result's exact value depends on how it was computed, which can leak the computation. The server multiplies by a fresh encryption of 0 before sending a result back: in part 1 of the script, \(683\) re-randomized three times gave 142, 1119 and 781, all of which decrypt to 3.

What the server cannot do is compute \(\mathrm{Enc}(m_1 m_2)\) from \(\mathrm{Enc}(m_1)\) and \(\mathrm{Enc}(m_2)\). No method is known. §9 explains why one should not expect one.

4. Goldwasser–Micali: XOR on encrypted bits#

Goldwasser and Micali's 1982 scheme encrypts single bits. It was the first encryption scheme with a proof of semantic security; the paper also introduced the notion.

Keys. \(n = pq\), and a number \(x\) that is a non-square both mod \(p\) and mod \(q\). For \(p, q \equiv 3 \pmod 4\), \(x = -1\) works. The secret key is the factorization; decryption needs only \(p\).

Encryption of a bit \(b\), with a random \(y\):

\[\mathrm{Enc}(b) = y^2 \cdot x^b \bmod n\]

An encryption of 0 is a random square; an encryption of 1 is a random non-square. Decryption checks whether \(c\) is a square mod \(p\) with Euler's criterion: \(c^{(p-1)/2} \equiv 1 \pmod p\) means \(b = 0\).

The homomorphism. \(\mathrm{Enc}(b_1) \cdot \mathrm{Enc}(b_2) = (y_1 y_2)^2 \cdot x^{b_1 + b_2}\). When both bits are 1, \(x^2\) is itself a square, so the product encrypts \(b_1 \oplus b_2\). Multiplying by \(x\) flips the bit, a free NOT.

Security rests on the quadratic residuosity problem: without the factors of \(n\), nobody knows how to tell squares from non-squares among the numbers whose Jacobi symbol is +1. The Jacobi symbol is a square test mod \(n\) that anyone can run without knowing \(p\) and \(q\); it only says "square mod both primes, or non-square mod both". Exactly half of the numbers that pass it are real squares (the encryptions of 0); the other half are the encryptions of 1. Mod 77, 30 of the 60 units pass, and 15 of those are squares.

With \(p = 7\), \(q = 11\), \(n = 77\), \(x = 76 = -1\), part 3 of the script gave:

  Enc(1) = 62, Enc(1) = 40:  62 * 40 mod 77 = 16  -> decrypts to 0   (1 XOR 1 = 0)

Check by hand: \(16 \bmod 7 = 2\) and \(2^3 = 8 \equiv 1 \pmod 7\), so 16 is a square: bit 0.

The price is size: every bit becomes a number mod \(n\), about 3072 bits at real key sizes. GM is historically central, but its homomorphism, XOR, is only the "addition" half of what circuits need (§6).

5. What one operation is good for#

  • Electronic voting. Each voter encrypts 1 (yes) or 0 (no), the tally server multiplies all ballots, and only the total is decrypted. P4 §5 does this with exponential ElGamal, as Helios does, and lists what real systems add. Part 2 of our script runs the same tally with Paillier, and its cheater who submits \(\mathrm{Enc}(5)\) shifts the count by 5 unnoticed: that is what the per-ballot zero-knowledge proofs are for.
  • Private aggregation. Google's Private Join and Compute (open-sourced 2019) lets two parties compute how many identifiers they have in common and the sum of the values attached to the shared ones (for example, purchases that followed an ad), without revealing the other records. It combines private set intersection with Paillier-style additive encryption (see 08).
  • Standards. ISO/IEC 18033-6:2019, from the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) and the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC), standardizes exponential ElGamal and Paillier (07 §5).

PHE is cheap: one modular multiplication or exponentiation per operation, with mature implementations. Whenever a computation is genuinely linear, PHE is often the right tool.

Scheme Year Plaintext operation Ciphertext operation Security rests on Catch
One-time pad mod \(n\) classic \(+\) add mod \(n\) nothing (perfect secrecy) key as long as the data, secret-key only
Textbook RSA 1978 \(\times\) multiply mod \(n\) RSA problem deterministic, not IND-CPA
Goldwasser–Micali 1982 XOR multiply mod \(n\) quadratic residuosity one bit per \(\lvert n \rvert\)-bit ciphertext
ElGamal 1985 \(\times\) componentwise multiply DDH
Exponential ElGamal \(+\) componentwise multiply DDH decrypting needs a small discrete log
Paillier 1999 \(+\), \(\times\) constant multiply mod \(n^2\), power DCR ciphertext twice the modulus size

6. Why one operation is not enough: computation as circuits#

A server holding ciphertexts cannot run a program the normal way. It cannot see a branch condition, a loop counter or an array index, because those are encrypted too. All it can do is apply the scheme's operations in a pattern fixed in advance. That pattern is a circuit: a directed acyclic graph of gates, with the inputs at the leaves, whose shape does not depend on the data.

XOR and AND are enough#

On bits, XOR is addition mod 2 and AND is multiplication mod 2. With the constant 1 they give every other logic gate:

\[\lnot x = x \oplus 1, \qquad x \lor y = x \oplus y \oplus xy, \qquad \mathrm{NAND}(x, y) = 1 \oplus xy\]

NAND (not-and) alone is universal, so \(\{\oplus, \wedge\}\) is too. Algebraically: every Boolean function of \(k\) bits is a polynomial over \(\mathbb{Z}_2\) (its algebraic normal form). Any program that runs for a bounded number of steps compiles to such a circuit. So a scheme that can add and multiply encrypted bits, as often as needed, can compute anything. Goldwasser–Micali has the XOR and lacks the AND; Paillier adds and cannot multiply.

Example: a 4-bit adder. A full adder computes the sum bit and the carry:

\[s = a \oplus b \oplus c_{\text{in}}, \qquad c_{\text{out}} = ab \oplus c_{\text{in}}\,(a \oplus b)\]

(The two products are never both 1, so XOR acts as OR.) One half adder (1 XOR, 1 AND) for bit 0 and three full adders (3 XOR, 2 AND each) for bits 1–3 take 10 XOR and 7 AND gates. Part 4 of the script checks it on all 256 input pairs.

Arithmetic circuits#

Bits are not the only option. With gates "\(+\) mod \(t\)" and "\(\times\) mod \(t\)", one ciphertext can hold a whole number mod \(t\), and integer arithmetic becomes native. This is how the Brakerski/Fan–Vercauteren (BFV) and Brakerski–Gentry–Vaikuntanathan (BGV) schemes work (and the Cheon–Kim–Kim–Song scheme, CKKS, approximately, for real numbers). Over a prime \(t\), every function \(\mathbb{Z}_t \to \mathbb{Z}_t\) is a polynomial (Lagrange interpolation), but often one of degree close to \(t\), which is expensive (below).

An if becomes a select#

if c:
    r = x
else:
    r = y

With \(c \in \{0, 1\}\) encrypted, the server computes both branches and blends them:

\[r = c \cdot x + (1 - c) \cdot y = y + c \cdot (x - y)\]

One multiplication selects the result. On bits it is \(r_i = y_i \oplus c\,(x_i \oplus y_i)\) per bit, a multiplexer. The cost of an if is the cost of both branches plus the select. The script's part 4 computes \(\max(9, 12)\) as select(9 > 12, 9, 12): 23 XOR, 14 AND, depth 4.

Loops need public bounds#

while x > 0: ... cannot stop early, because the server does not know when the condition turns false. The loop is unrolled to a public maximum number of iterations, and each iteration's effect is applied through a select: state = select(cond, step(state), state). The bound must cover the worst case. Array access with an encrypted index works the same way: \(a[i] = \sum_j [i = j] \cdot a[j]\) touches every element, which is the price of not revealing \(i\). It is the basis of private information retrieval (PIR): fetching a database entry without revealing which one (../deep_dives/bfv_pir.md).

Comparisons are expensive#

Comparing two \(k\)-bit numbers bit by bit takes about \(6k\) gates (roughly \(4k\) XOR and \(2k\) AND). Scanned as a chain, every bit adds one AND to the critical path. Merged as a balanced tree, the depth drops to \(\log_2 k + 1\) at the cost of about 50 % more AND gates (\(3k - 2\) instead of \(2k - 1\)). The script's counts (part 4):

Bits \(k\) Chain: AND gates Chain: depth Tree: AND gates Tree: depth
4 7 4 10 3
16 31 16 46 5
64 127 64 190 7

In arithmetic circuits comparisons are worse. Equality mod a prime \(t\) uses Fermat's little theorem, \(d^{t-1} = 1\) for \(d \ne 0\):

\[[x = y] = 1 - (x - y)^{t-1} \pmod t\]

For \(t = 17\) that is four squarings, depth 4. For \(t = 65537 = 2^{16} + 1\) it is depth 16, and "less than" is worse still. This is why ReLU, max-pooling and sorting dominate the cost of encrypted machine learning, and why the programmable bootstrapping of torus fully homomorphic encryption (TFHE), which evaluates a lookup table as a side effect, is attractive (05 §5).

7. Multiplicative depth#

The multiplicative depth of a circuit is the largest number of multiplication gates (ciphertext times ciphertext) on any path from an input to an output. Additions and multiplications by public constants do not count.

It is the cost measure of HE because of noise. In every practical FHE scheme, a ciphertext carries a small random error that grows a little with each addition and a lot with each multiplication (P7, P8). Parallel multiplications do not compound; sequential ones do. So the parameters must be sized for the depth, and the number of gates matters much less.

Both use 7 multiplications. A depth-\(d\) circuit computes polynomials of degree at most \(2^d\), since each level can at most double the degree. So \(x^{16}\) needs depth 4 (repeated squaring), and nothing of degree 9 fits in depth 3. FHE compilers rebalance circuits into trees exactly like this, as the comparison table above shows: 64-bit comparison drops from depth 64 to depth 7. 8. PHE, SHE, LHE and FHE#

Class Definition Examples
PHE (partially homomorphic) One operation, \(+\) or \(\times\), any number of times RSA, ElGamal, Goldwasser–Micali, Paillier
SHE (somewhat homomorphic) Both \(+\) and \(\times\), but only for a limited class of circuits, typically low-degree polynomials; the limit is built into the scheme Boneh–Goh–Nissim (2005): any number of additions and one multiplication; Gentry's 2009 scheme before bootstrapping
LHE (leveled) For any depth \(L\) chosen at key generation, parameters exist that evaluate every circuit of depth at most \(L\). Parameters and ciphertext size grow with \(L\), not with the size of the circuit BGV, BFV, CKKS without bootstrapping: most real deployments
FHE (fully homomorphic) One fixed parameter set evaluates every circuit, of any size and depth A leveled scheme plus bootstrapping (P9)

The definitions of LHE and FHE come with a requirement that rules out cheating: compactness (the PHE schemes above have it automatically, since their ciphertexts have a fixed size). The output ciphertext must not grow with the circuit, or else the "AES cheat" of chapter 01, which encrypts with the Advanced Encryption Standard (AES), staples a description of \(f\) to the ciphertext and lets the client do the work, would qualify as FHE. 01 §2, Why compactness matters explains it. Leveled schemes satisfy a slightly relaxed form, since their sizes depend on \(L\).

In practice "FHE" is used loosely for the whole lattice family, even for deployments that never bootstrap (01 §3).

9. Why it took from 1978 to 2009#

In 1978, the year RSA was published, Rivest, Adleman and Dertouzos asked for "privacy homomorphisms": encryption that lets an untrusted party compute on data. Several of their candidate schemes were broken within a decade (Brickell and Yacobi, 1987). Later designs that offered both operations fared no better: a 2002 scheme by Domingo-Ferrer fell to known-plaintext attacks (Wagner 2003, using linear algebra, and independently Cheon and Nam). Computation on encrypted data without compactness did appear (Sander, Young and Yung 1999 handled shallow circuits, with ciphertexts that grow exponentially in the depth). The secure, compact schemes kept one operation. Why?

The second operation is the hard problem. In exponential ElGamal and Paillier the message sits in an exponent: \(g^m\) or \((1+n)^m\). Multiplying group elements adds exponents, which gives addition for free. Multiplying two hidden exponents means computing \(g^{ab}\) from \(g^a\) and \(g^b\), the computational Diffie–Hellman problem of P4 §3. For exponential ElGamal the argument is exact. Suppose an algorithm Mult turned \(\mathrm{Enc}(a), \mathrm{Enc}(b)\) into \(\mathrm{Enc}(ab)\). Given \(g^a\) and \(g^b\), anyone could make their own key pair, wrap the two values as ciphertexts \((1, g^a)\) and \((1, g^b)\) (exponential ElGamal encryptions of \(a\) and \(b\) with randomness \(y = 0\)), run Mult, and decrypt the result to \(g^{ab}\). That solves CDH, and solving CDH decrypts ElGamal without the key. The structure that gives one operation for free makes the other as hard as breaking the scheme.

Pairings buy exactly one multiplication. A bilinear pairing is a map \(e: G \times G \to G_T\) with \(e(g^a, g^b) = e(g, g)^{ab}\): it multiplies exponents, but its output lands in a different group \(G_T\) where no further pairing applies. Boneh, Goh and Nissim (2005) built on this a scheme with unlimited additions and exactly one multiplication, enough to evaluate quadratic polynomials (their paper's "2-DNF formulas"). Decryption needs a small discrete logarithm, so messages must be small.

What changed: noise. Lattice-based encryption (P7) hides the message by adding a small random error, not by putting it in an exponent. Numbers with small errors can be added and multiplied, and the errors stay manageable for a while. That gives somewhat homomorphic encryption almost for free. The obstacle is that the errors grow until they swamp the message. Gentry's 2009 thesis showed how to reset them, called bootstrapping (P9), and that turned SHE into the first FHE. His first scheme used ideal lattices; since 2011 the schemes are built on Regev's LWE problem (2005) and its ring version. That mathematics is what P7 builds.

11. How this connects to homomorphic encryption#

  • Algebra keeps one operation. Public-key schemes built on algebra keep exactly one operation. They are useful (voting, aggregation) and standardized, but they are not enough.
  • Circuits are the programming model of FHE. Every encrypted program is a fixed circuit of additions and multiplications: branches become selects, loops get public bounds, comparisons get expensive. Compilers such as those in 05 §9 do this translation.
  • Depth is the budget. Noise grows with every sequential multiplication. Leveled schemes are sized for a depth \(L\) (P8), and bootstrapping (P9) removes the limit.
  • The one-time pad is the template. P7 builds LWE encryption as a one-time pad whose pad is generated from a short key plus a little noise. It adds as easily as the pad of §2, and P8 makes it multiply.

12. Check yourself#

  1. A Paillier server holds \(c = \mathrm{Enc}(m)\) under public key \(n\). How does it compute an encryption of \(3m + 5\), and what should it do before sending the result back?
    Show answer

    \(c^3 \cdot (1+n)^5 \bmod n^2\) (or \(c^3 \cdot \mathrm{Enc}(5)\)). Then re-randomize by multiplying with a fresh \(\mathrm{Enc}(0) = r^n\), so the ciphertext does not reveal how it was computed.

  2. Textbook RSA is multiplicatively homomorphic. Give two separate reasons why it is not used for computing on private data.
    Show answer

    It is deterministic, so not IND-CPA secure: equal messages give equal ciphertexts and anyone can test guesses. And it offers only multiplication; the padding that makes RSA secure (OAEP) removes even that.

  3. In Goldwasser–Micali you hold \(c_1 = \mathrm{Enc}(b_1)\) and \(c_2 = \mathrm{Enc}(b_2)\). Compute an encryption of \(\lnot(b_1 \oplus b_2)\). Can you compute \(b_1 \wedge b_2\)?
    Show answer

    \(c_1 \cdot c_2 \cdot x \bmod n\): the product gives the XOR, and the factor \(x\) flips the bit. AND would need a multiplication of bits, which GM does not offer and nobody knows how to add compactly.

  4. What is the smallest multiplicative depth for computing \(x^8 \cdot y\)? Why can it not be 3?
    Show answer

    Depth 4: \(x^2, x^4, x^8\) take three squarings, then one more multiplication by \(y\). Depth 3 can only reach degree \(2^3 = 8\), and \(x^8 y\) has degree 9.

  5. Over \(\mathbb{Z}_{17}\), write z = a if x == y else b as an arithmetic circuit. What is its depth?
    Show answer

    \(c = 1 - (x - y)^{16}\) (four squarings, depth 4), then \(z = b + c \cdot (a - b)\) (one more multiplication). Depth 5.

  6. Why would an efficient way to multiply plaintexts under exponential ElGamal be bad news for exponential ElGamal itself?
    Show answer

    It would solve the computational Diffie–Hellman problem in that group: wrap \(g^a\) and \(g^b\) as ciphertexts under your own key, multiply, decrypt to \(g^{ab}\). And CDH is exactly what protects ElGamal: anyone who can solve it computes \(h^y\) from \(h\) and \(g^y\) and decrypts.

13. Key terms#

Term Meaning
Homomorphism A map that respects an operation: \(f(a \circ b) = f(a) * f(b)\)
Homomorphic encryption Encryption whose decryption is a homomorphism: operating on ciphertexts operates on the plaintexts
Partially homomorphic (PHE) Supports one operation, any number of times
Paillier Additively homomorphic public-key scheme mod \(n^2\), security from DCR
Goldwasser–Micali Bit encryption by squares and non-squares; multiplying ciphertexts XORs the bits
Re-randomization Multiplying (or adding) an encryption of the neutral element to get a fresh-looking ciphertext of the same message
Circuit A fixed, data-independent graph of gates; the form every homomorphic computation takes
Arithmetic circuit A circuit of \(+\) and \(\times\) gates mod \(t\) instead of bit gates
Select (multiplexer) \(c \cdot x + (1 - c) \cdot y\): the branch-free replacement for if
Multiplicative depth Largest number of ciphertext multiplications on any input-to-output path
Somewhat homomorphic (SHE) Both operations, but only for a limited class of circuits
Leveled homomorphic (LHE) Any circuit up to a depth \(L\) chosen at key generation
Fully homomorphic (FHE) Any circuit with one fixed parameter set; compact
Compactness The output ciphertext's size does not depend on the evaluated circuit
Bilinear pairing A map with \(e(g^a, g^b) = e(g,g)^{ab}\); gives BGN its single multiplication

P5 · What "secure" means: attack models and IND-CPA#

← P4 Public-key cryptography · index · next: P6 Computing on ciphertexts →

Every homomorphic encryption paper says "IND-CPA secure under RLWE" (indistinguishability under chosen-plaintext attack, assuming ring learning with errors is hard). This chapter unpacks both halves of that phrase. IND-CPA is a game that no efficient attacker may win noticeably more often than a coin flip; "under RLWE" is a reduction that turns any winning attacker into a solver for a lattice problem. On the way you will see why encryption must be randomized, why homomorphic encryption (HE) can never reach the stronger CCA2 notion (security against adaptive chosen-ciphertext attack), and what the definition leaves out. Chapter 01 has a one-page summary (01 §2); this is the long, gentle version.

In this chapter you will learn to:

  • name the five classic attacker models and give a real attack for each;
  • say what the security parameter λ, "efficient", "negligible" and "128-bit security" mean;
  • play the IND-CPA game, compute an adversary's advantage, and show that deterministic encryption always loses;
  • state semantic security and explain why it is equivalent to IND-CPA;
  • read "secure under DDH" (decisional Diffie–Hellman) or "secure under LWE" (learning with errors) as a proof by reduction, and sketch the one for ElGamal;
  • explain why every homomorphic scheme fails IND-CCA2 (indistinguishability under adaptive chosen-ciphertext attack), and list what IND-CPA does not cover.

Companion script: code/primer/p5_ind_cpa_game.py. Run it from the homomorphic_encryption/ folder with python3 code/primer/p5_ind_cpa_game.py. Its output is split into numbered parts, and the text says which part shows what.

1. Why "secure" needs a definition#

Try to say what a secure encryption scheme is, and the obvious attempts fail:

  • "The attacker cannot find the key." The scheme \(\mathrm{Enc}_k(m) = m\) never reveals the key.
  • "The attacker cannot recover the message." A scheme that leaks the first half of every message passes.
  • "The attacker cannot recover any single bit of the message." A scheme can protect every bit and still leak whether your salary is above $100,000, or whether two messages are equal.

What we want is stronger: the ciphertext reveals nothing about the message beyond its length, even to an attacker who already knows a lot (say, that the message is either "yes" or "no"). P1's perfect secrecy said exactly this for attackers with unlimited computing power, and paid for it with keys as long as the data. Modern definitions keep the idea and relax two things: the attacker is efficient, and it may have a negligible edge.

Goldwasser and Micali made this precise in 1982. A modern security definition has three parts:

  1. What the attacker can do: what it sees and what it may ask for (§2).
  2. How much work it may spend (§3).
  3. What counts as a win (§4).

A notion's name combines a goal with a power: IND-CPA is indistinguishability under chosen-plaintext attack, IND-CCA2 is indistinguishability under adaptive chosen-ciphertext attack.

2. Attacker models: what the attacker can do#

From weakest to strongest attacker:

Model The attacker gets A real example
Ciphertext-only (COA) Ciphertexts only Frequency analysis of substitution and Vigenère ciphers (P1)
Known-plaintext (KPA) Some plaintext–ciphertext pairs it did not choose Bletchley Park's "cribs" (predictable phrases such as weather reports) against Enigma; the two-time pad (P1)
Chosen-plaintext (CPA) Encryptions of messages of its choice Midway 1942; BEAST against Transport Layer Security (TLS) 1.0 (2011); every public-key scheme
Non-adaptive chosen-ciphertext (CCA1) Decryptions of ciphertexts of its choice, but only before it sees the target "Lunchtime" access to a decryption device; key recovery on lattice-based homomorphic schemes (§7)
Adaptive chosen-ciphertext (CCA2) Decryptions of anything except the target, before and after seeing it Bleichenbacher's attack on Rivest–Shamir–Adleman (RSA) encryption with Public-Key Cryptography Standards (PKCS) #1 v1.5 padding in SSL (1998), still found in many servers by ROBOT (2018)

Some detail on the stronger three:

  • Chosen plaintext. In May 1942 US Navy codebreakers suspected that "AF" in Japanese traffic meant Midway. Midway was told to report, in plain language, that its water supply had broken down; a decrypted Japanese message soon reported that "AF" was short of water. The codebreakers had chosen part of a plaintext and watched it being encrypted. In 2011 the BEAST attack had JavaScript in the victim's browser choose plaintexts inside a TLS 1.0 connection, whose cipher block chaining (CBC) mode used predictable initialization vectors (IVs; P3). For public-key encryption CPA is free: the public key is an encryption machine anyone can use. So CPA is the weakest model that even makes sense for public-key schemes.
  • CCA1, the "lunchtime attack". The attacker can use a decryption box while its owner is at lunch, then must work alone. Loftus, May, Smart and Vercauteren (2011) showed that this is enough to recover the key of Gentry's original somewhat-homomorphic scheme (§7).
  • CCA2. The attacker can keep asking for decryptions after it has the target, as long as it does not submit the target itself. This sounds artificial, but servers do it all the time: they decrypt what clients send and react. Bleichenbacher's SSL servers answered differently when a decrypted message had invalid padding, and about a million crafted ciphertexts were enough to decrypt the key exchange of a recorded session. Twenty years later ROBOT found the same oracle in products from nine vendors and on almost a third of the top 100 web domains.

Each model contains the one above it, so a scheme secure against a stronger attacker is secure against every weaker one. The attacker's goal can also vary, from recovering the key (the hardest goal) through recovering a whole message down to telling two messages apart (the easiest). Security definitions pick the easiest goal and the strongest power they can afford: if even distinguishing is impossible, everything harder is impossible too.

3. How much work an attacker may do#

The security parameter#

Key generation takes a security parameter λ, written KeyGen(1^λ) in papers: λ in unary, so that "polynomial in the input length" means "polynomial in λ". λ is a knob. Turning it up makes keys longer and parameters larger, and everything else is measured as a function of it: honest parties' work, the attacker's work, the attacker's success probability. In HE, KeyGen(λ) picks the ring dimension N and modulus q (the size and modulus of the lattice problem, P7) from tables for λ = 128 (ch. 01 §2, 06 §2).

Efficient means probabilistic polynomial time#

The attacker is any probabilistic polynomial-time (PPT) algorithm: it may flip coins, and its running time is at most \(p(\lambda)\) for some polynomial \(p\). Polynomials are used because they are robust. A polynomial-time attacker that calls a polynomial-time subroutine polynomially many times is still polynomial time, and the class does not depend on the machine model. The attacker may use a far bigger polynomial than the honest parties (\(\lambda^{10}\) against \(\lambda^2\)). What makes cryptography possible is the gap between the polynomial work of using a scheme and the exponential work, like \(2^\lambda\), of breaking it.

Negligible functions#

A function \(\varepsilon(\lambda)\) is negligible if it eventually drops below the inverse of every polynomial: for every polynomial \(p\) there is a \(\lambda_0\) such that \(\varepsilon(\lambda) < 1/p(\lambda)\) for all \(\lambda > \lambda_0\).

  • \(2^{-\lambda}\), \(2^{-\sqrt{\lambda}}\) and \(\lambda^{-\log \lambda}\) are negligible.
  • \(\lambda^{-10}\) is not: it never drops below \(1/\lambda^{11}\).

The reason for this definition is closure. A polynomial times a negligible function is still negligible, so an attacker that repeats a negligible-chance attack polynomially many times still has a negligible chance. A non-negligible chance \(\varepsilon\), on the other hand, can be amplified when success is recognizable (a recovered key can be tested): about \(1/\varepsilon\) repetitions, a polynomial number, give a constant success rate.

Optional on a first read: the table and the paragraph after it. The definition is asymptotic, and the numbers show what that means:

λ \(2^{-\lambda}\) (negligible) \(\lambda^{-10}\) (not negligible) \(2^{-\sqrt{\lambda}}\) (negligible)
20 \(9.5 \times 10^{-7}\) \(9.8 \times 10^{-14}\) 0.045
58 \(3.5 \times 10^{-18}\) \(2.3 \times 10^{-18}\) 0.0051
59 \(1.7 \times 10^{-18}\) \(2.0 \times 10^{-18}\) 0.0049
128 \(2.9 \times 10^{-39}\) \(8.5 \times 10^{-22}\) 0.00039

From λ = 2 up to λ = 58 the non-negligible \(\lambda^{-10}\) is the smaller one. From λ = 59 on, \(2^{-\lambda}\) wins, by more every step. And \(2^{-\sqrt{\lambda}}\) is negligible, yet at λ = 128 it is one chance in 2,500. Negligible is about the trend, not about any particular key size.

Concrete security: what "128-bit security" means#

Practitioners therefore also use concrete security. A scheme instance has κ-bit security if every known attack needs about \(2^\kappa\) operations to succeed; more carefully, an attacker that runs in time \(t\) and succeeds with probability \(\varepsilon\) has roughly \(t / \varepsilon \ge 2^\kappa\). Guessing a 128-bit key on the first try (\(t = 1\), \(\varepsilon = 2^{-128}\)) and searching all keys (\(t = 2^{128}\), \(\varepsilon = 1\)) both cost \(2^{128}\) in this sense. P1 explained what \(2^{128}\) operations mean physically.

In practice λ is set to the target: pick λ = 128 and choose parameters so that the best known attacks cost at least \(2^{128}\). That means the Advanced Encryption Standard with 128-bit keys (AES-128), RSA-3072 or a 256-bit elliptic curve (P4 §8), and for HE the ring dimension and modulus from the HomomorphicEncryption.org tables, checked with the Lattice Estimator (06 §2).

4. Security as a game: IND-CPA#

The game#

  1. The challenger generates the keys, flips a secret coin \(b\), and gives the adversary pk. For a secret-key scheme it gives an encryption oracle instead: a box that encrypts any message the adversary submits.
  2. The adversary picks two messages \(m_0, m_1\) of the same length. It chooses them, so it can pick the pair that is easiest to tell apart.
  3. The challenger returns the challenge \(c^* = \mathrm{Enc}(pk, m_b)\).
  4. The adversary may keep computing and encrypting, then outputs a guess \(b'\).

Equal lengths are required because a scheme cannot hide the length of an arbitrarily long message. Length leaks are real, though: the CRIME attack on TLS (2012) read secrets from the length of compressed-then-encrypted data. The definition simply declares them out of scope.

The adversary's advantage measures its edge over guessing:

\[\mathrm{Adv}^{\text{IND-CPA}}(\mathcal{A}) = \Big\lvert \Pr[b' = b] - \tfrac{1}{2} \Big\rvert\]

A scheme is IND-CPA secure if every PPT adversary has negligible advantage. A few remarks:

  • Probability \(\tfrac12\) is free: a coin flip gets it. Advantage counts only what the ciphertext gave away.
  • An adversary that is right with probability 0 has advantage \(\tfrac12\): flipping its answers makes it always right. That is why the definition uses the absolute value.
  • Some books define the advantage as \(\lvert \Pr[b' = 1 \mid b = 1] - \Pr[b' = 1 \mid b = 0] \rvert\), which is exactly twice ours. Same notion, different scale.
  • The game gives the attacker everything except \(b\): the public key, the choice of messages, as much encryption as it likes. If it still cannot tell which of its own two messages was encrypted, it cannot learn any property in which they differ.

Deterministic encryption always loses#

Here is an adversary that needs no cleverness:

  1. Pick any \(m_0 \ne m_1\).
  2. On receiving \(c^*\), compute \(\mathrm{Enc}(pk, m_0)\) yourself.
  3. Output \(b' = 0\) if it equals \(c^*\), otherwise \(b' = 1\).

If encryption is deterministic, \(\mathrm{Enc}(pk, m_0) = c^*\) exactly when \(b = 0\), so the adversary is always right: advantage \(\tfrac12\), the maximum. By hand with textbook RSA (\(n = 3233\), \(e = 17\), P4 §4): the adversary picks \(m_0 = 2\), \(m_1 = 3\). The challenger flips \(b = 1\) and sends \(c^* = 3^{17} \bmod 3233 = 1211\). The adversary computes \(\mathrm{Enc}(2) = 1752 \ne 1211\) and answers \(b' = 1\). Correct, and it would have been correct for \(b = 0\) too.

So no deterministic public-key scheme is IND-CPA secure, however hard its underlying problem. For secret-key schemes the same adversary uses the encryption oracle. Three consequences:

  1. Encryption must be randomized, or at least stateful: nonce-based symmetric encryption, such as counter mode (CTR) and Galois/counter mode (GCM) (P3 §6), gets its variation from a nonce that must never repeat, and its version of the game forbids the adversary from repeating nonces.
  2. Ciphertexts must expand. Each message needs many possible ciphertexts, so ciphertexts are longer than messages: twice as long for ElGamal, much more for LWE (P7).
  3. Determinism also leaks inside one message. Electronic codebook (ECB) mode (P3) encrypts equal blocks to equal blocks. An adversary that submits \(m_0\) = ABAB (two equal 2-byte blocks) and \(m_1\) = ABCD only has to check whether the two halves of \(c^*\) are equal. It does not even need the oracle.

Randomized encryption: this adversary learns nothing#

Against ElGamal the same adversary fails. Its own \(\mathrm{Enc}(m_0)\) uses a fresh random \(y\), so it matches \(c^*\) only with probability about \(1/q\), whatever \(b\) is. Its guess is a coin flip. In part 3 of the script it wins 49.6 % of 2,000 games, inside the sampling noise of about ±2.2 %.

That is not a proof of security, and no simulation can be. The definition talks about every efficient adversary, and a script can only try a few. One winning adversary proves a scheme insecure; security needs a proof (§6). The next example shows why the difference matters.

A subtle failure: ElGamal in the wrong group#

Randomization is necessary, not sufficient. Run ElGamal in all of \(\mathbb{Z}_p^*\), with \(g\) a generator of the whole group, instead of in the subgroup of squares (P4 §3). The tool is the Legendre symbol: by Euler's criterion \(x^{(p-1)/2} \bmod p\) is \(+1\) if \(x\) is a square and \(-1\) otherwise, and the symbol is multiplicative. A generator of the whole group is a non-square, so the symbol of \(g^k\) is \((-1)^k\): it reveals the parity of \(k\). The adversary:

  1. picks \(m_0\) a square and \(m_1\) a non-square;
  2. reads the parity of \(x\) from the symbol of \(h = g^x\), and the parity of \(y\) from the symbol of \(c_1 = g^y\);
  3. computes the symbol of the pad \(h^y = g^{xy}\), which is \((-1)^{xy}\);
  4. divides it out of the symbol of \(c_2 = m_b h^y\). The result is the symbol of \(m_b\): \(+1\) means \(m_0\), \(-1\) means \(m_1\).

By hand with \(p = 23\) and \(g = 5\), which generates all 22 non-zero residues: Bob's \(x = 3\) gives \(h = 5^3 = 125 \equiv 10\), a non-square, so \(x\) is odd. The adversary picks \(m_0 = 2\) (a square, \(5^2 \equiv 2\)) and \(m_1 = 5\) (a non-square). The challenger flips \(b = 0\), picks \(y = 7\) and sends \(c^* = (5^7,\ 2 \cdot 10^7) \equiv (17, 5)\). The adversary sees that 17 is a non-square, so \(y\) is odd and the pad's symbol is \((-1)^{\text{odd} \cdot \text{odd}} = -1\). Then \(c_2 = 5\) has symbol \(-1\), and \((-1)/(-1) = +1\): the message is a square, so \(b' = 0\). Correct, and it always will be.

In the subgroup of squares every element has symbol \(+1\), so the same adversary sees nothing: it always answers 0 and wins exactly when the coin is 0 (script, part 3: 50.1 %). This is DDH failing in \(\mathbb{Z}_p^*\), and it is why the proof in §6 needs a group in which DDH is believed hard.

5. Semantic security#

Optional on a first read. Chapter 01 needs only the conclusion of this section: semantic security and IND-CPA are equivalent, so "IND-CPA" really means "the ciphertext reveals nothing".

IND-CPA is a game; the property we actually want sounds different. Semantic security (Goldwasser and Micali) says: whatever an efficient attacker can compute about the message from the ciphertext, it could compute without the ciphertext. Formally, for every PPT adversary \(\mathcal{A}\) there is a PPT simulator \(\mathcal{S}\) that sees only pk and the length of the message, such that for every efficiently samplable message distribution, every target function \(f\) and every piece of side information \(\mathrm{aux}(m)\) (what the attacker knew beforehand):

\[\Big\lvert \Pr\big[\mathcal{A}(pk, \mathrm{Enc}(pk, m), \mathrm{aux}(m)) = f(m)\big] - \Pr\big[\mathcal{S}(pk, \lvert m \rvert, \mathrm{aux}(m)) = f(m)\big] \Big\rvert \le \mathrm{negl}(\lambda)\]

It is the computational version of P1's perfect secrecy: seeing the ciphertext does not change what an efficient attacker can work out. Goldwasser and Micali proved (1984) that their indistinguishability notion implies semantic security; Micali, Rackoff and Sloan (1988) showed the converse, so semantic security and IND-CPA are equivalent. The intuition for each direction:

  • Semantic ⇒ IND-CPA. Suppose \(\mathcal{A}\) tells \(\mathrm{Enc}(m_0)\) from \(\mathrm{Enc}(m_1)\) with advantage \(\varepsilon\). Take the message distribution "\(m_0\) or \(m_1\), each with probability \(\tfrac12\)" and \(f(m)\) = "which one". \(\mathcal{A}\) (with its answers flipped if it is reliably wrong) computes \(f\) with probability \(\tfrac12 + \varepsilon\). A simulator without the ciphertext has no information about the choice and cannot beat \(\tfrac12\). So semantic security fails.
  • IND-CPA ⇒ semantic. Given any \(\mathcal{A}\), build the simulator: encrypt a dummy message \(0 \dots 0\) of the right length yourself and run \(\mathcal{A}\) on that. If \(\mathcal{A}\) computed \(f(m)\) noticeably better from \(\mathrm{Enc}(m)\) than from \(\mathrm{Enc}(0 \dots 0)\), it would be a distinguisher between encryptions of \(m\) and of \(0 \dots 0\): an IND-CPA adversary that samples \(m\) itself, submits \(m_0 = m\) and \(m_1 = 0 \dots 0\), and guesses "\(m_0\)" when \(\mathcal{A}\)'s output equals \(f(m)\). IND-CPA rules that out, so the simulator does as well as \(\mathcal{A}\).

Papers use IND-CPA because a one-bit guessing game is far easier to analyse; semantic security is the reassurance that the game captures what we care about. One more useful fact: for public-key schemes, security for one challenge implies security for many challenges, with a loss proportional to their number (a "hybrid argument"). Encrypting a million values is no weaker than encrypting one.

6. Proofs by reduction#

The shape of a reduction#

Nobody can prove that a practical scheme is secure outright: that would prove one-way functions exist, and with them P ≠ NP (P4 §2). What cryptographers prove instead is a reduction:

If problem X is hard, then scheme S is IND-CPA secure.

The proof goes by contraposition. Take any efficient adversary \(\mathcal{A}\) with advantage \(\varepsilon\) against S, and build from it an efficient algorithm \(\mathcal{B}\) that solves X with advantage related to \(\varepsilon\). If X is hard, \(\varepsilon\) must be negligible. In a picture, \(\mathcal{B}\) wraps \(\mathcal{A}\) and plays the challenger for it:

                      instance of problem X
                               |
                               v
   +---------------------- B (the reduction) -----------------------+
   |   builds a public key from the instance  --------->  +------+  |
   |                                                      |  A   |  |
   |   <------------------------------------- m0, m1 ---  |      |  |
   |   builds c* from the instance and m_b  ----------->  |      |  |
   |   <----------------------------------------- b' ---  +------+  |
   |   answers X using whether A guessed right                      |
   +----------------------------------------------------------------+

\(\mathcal{B}\) has three obligations: its simulation must look exactly like the real game to \(\mathcal{A}\) (or \(\mathcal{A}\) might behave differently), \(\mathcal{A}\)'s success must translate into \(\mathcal{B}\)'s, and \(\mathcal{B}\) must be efficient. A reduction that loses a large factor, say an advantage of \(\varepsilon / 2^{20}\), is called loose, and the parameters need extra margin. And a reduction says nothing about attacks the model leaves out (§8).

ElGamal is IND-CPA secure if DDH is hard#

\(\mathcal{B}\) receives a DDH instance \((g, X = g^x, Y = g^y, Z)\), where either \(Z = g^{xy}\) (a "real" Diffie–Hellman triple) or \(Z = g^z\) for a random \(z\). It does not know which. It plays the challenger:

  1. Give \(\mathcal{A}\) the public key \(h = X\).
  2. \(\mathcal{A}\) outputs \(m_0, m_1\). Flip a coin \(b\) and send \(c^* = (Y,\ m_b \cdot Z)\).
  3. \(\mathcal{A}\) outputs \(b'\). Answer "real" if \(b' = b\), otherwise "random".
  • If \(Z = g^{xy}\): \(c^* = (g^y,\ m_b h^y)\) is exactly an ElGamal encryption of \(m_b\) with randomness \(y\). \(\mathcal{A}\) is in the real game and wins with probability \(\tfrac12 \pm \varepsilon\).
  • If \(Z\) is random: \(Z\) is a uniform group element independent of everything else, so \(m_b \cdot Z\) is uniform whatever \(b\) is. That is a one-time pad in the group (P1). \(\mathcal{A}\)'s view does not depend on \(b\), and it wins with probability exactly \(\tfrac12\).

So \(\mathcal{B}\) says "real" with probability \(\tfrac12 \pm \varepsilon\) on real triples and exactly \(\tfrac12\) on random ones. The gap between those two probabilities, \(\varepsilon\), is its advantage at DDH, bought at the cost of one extra multiplication. If DDH is hard, \(\varepsilon\) is negligible and ElGamal is IND-CPA secure. (The converse holds too: Tsiounis and Yung showed in 1998 that ElGamal's semantic security is equivalent to DDH.)

The proof in one line: DDH says the pad \(h^y\) looks random, and a random pad is a one-time pad.

LWE-based encryption is IND-CPA secure if decision-LWE is hard#

Preview of P7. An LWE ciphertext is a pair \((a, b)\) where \(a\) is a random vector mod \(q\) and \(b = \langle a, s \rangle + \Delta \cdot m + e \pmod q\). Here \(s\) is the secret key (a vector), \(\Delta\) is a large constant that lifts the message into the top bits, and \(e\) is a small random error, the noise. Decision-LWE (stated precisely below) says such pairs look uniformly random. P7 builds all of this slowly; here only its shape matters.

The same template gives the security of the LWE scheme of P7 §5 and ../code/toy_lwe.py, whose ciphertexts are \((a,\ \langle a, s \rangle + \Delta m + e)\). Decision-LWE says that samples \((a_i,\ \langle a_i, s \rangle + e_i)\) with small noise \(e_i\) cannot be told apart from uniform pairs \((a_i, u_i)\). \(\mathcal{B}\) gets a supply of samples of one kind or the other and plays the challenger:

  • For each encryption query \(m\), it takes a fresh sample \((a, u)\) and returns \((a,\ u + \Delta m)\).
  • For the challenge, it does the same with \(m_b\).

With LWE samples this is exactly the real scheme. With uniform samples every \(u + \Delta m\) is uniform whatever \(m\) is, a one-time pad again, and \(\mathcal{A}\) wins with probability exactly \(\tfrac12\). So \(\mathcal{B}\) inherits \(\mathcal{A}\)'s advantage. The public-key version (Regev's scheme, P7 §7) needs one more step, because the public key itself must first be shown to look random.

Part 3 of the script shows the other side of this proof. Remove the noise and the pad \(\langle a, s \rangle\) is no longer pseudorandom: 16 encryptions of 0 give 16 linear equations in s, Gaussian elimination solves them, and the adversary wins 100 % of the games. With noise in \([-20, 20]\) the same elimination returns garbage and the adversary is back to 49 %. The noise is what makes LWE secure (P7), and later what limits computation (P8, P9).

When an HE paper says "IND-CPA secure under RLWE", it means a reduction of exactly this shape, plus the circular security assumption for the evaluation keys, above all the bootstrapping key: they are encryptions of the secret key (or of functions of it, such as \(s^2\)) under itself, which the reduction does not cover (P9 §7, 02 §7).

7. Chosen-ciphertext security and malleability#

The IND-CCA2 game#

IND-CCA2 is the IND-CPA game plus a decryption oracle: the adversary may submit any ciphertext except \(c^*\) itself, before and after the challenge, and gets its decryption. (In IND-CCA1 the oracle closes when the challenge arrives.) The one exclusion is necessary, since decrypting \(c^*\) would win trivially. As §2 showed, the oracle models servers that decrypt and react, so this is the standard target for ordinary encryption: RSA with optimal asymmetric encryption padding (RSA-OAEP), the Module-Lattice-Based Key-Encapsulation Mechanism (ML-KEM) and AES-GCM (with unique nonces) all aim for it.

Malleability breaks CCA2#

Against ElGamal, the adversary does not need to decrypt \(c^*\); a relative will do:

  1. Receive \(c^* = (c_1, c_2)\) and make a fresh \(\mathrm{Enc}(2) = (g^{y'}, 2h^{y'})\) with its own random \(y'\).
  2. Submit \(c' = c^* \cdot \mathrm{Enc}(2) = (c_1 g^{y'},\ 2 c_2 h^{y'})\). It differs from \(c^*\), so the oracle answers: \(2 m_b\).
  3. Divide by 2 and compare with \(m_0\).

By hand, with the P4 key (\(p = 23\), \(g = 2\), \(x = 3\), \(h = 8\)): the adversary picks \(m_0 = 3\) and \(m_1 = 4\). The challenger flips \(b = 0\) and sends \(c^* = (16, 6)\), the encryption of 3 from P4 §5. The adversary picks \(y' = 2\), makes \(\mathrm{Enc}(2) = (2^2,\ 2 \cdot 8^2) = (4, 128) \equiv (4, 13)\) and submits \(c' = (16 \cdot 4,\ 6 \cdot 13) = (64, 78) \equiv (18, 9)\). The oracle computes \(18^3 \equiv 13\), \(13^{-1} \equiv 16\), \(9 \cdot 16 = 144 \equiv 6\) and returns 6. The adversary computes \(6 \cdot 2^{-1} = 6 \cdot 12 = 72 \equiv 3 = m_0\) and answers \(b' = 0\). It wins every time.

The principle is general. If anyone can turn \(c^*\) into a different ciphertext whose plaintext is a known, invertible function of \(m\), the scheme is not IND-CCA2 secure. A homomorphic scheme provides exactly such operations, by definition:

Scheme Mauled ciphertext Its plaintext
Textbook RSA \(c^* \cdot 2^e \bmod n\) \(2m\)
ElGamal \(c^* \cdot \mathrm{Enc}(2)\), or just \(c^* \cdot \mathrm{Enc}(1)\) \(2m\), or \(m\) itself
LWE, the Brakerski/Fan–Vercauteren scheme (BFV), the Cheon–Kim–Kim–Song scheme (CKKS) (P7–P8) \(c^* + \mathrm{Enc}(1)\), or just \(c^* + \mathrm{Enc}(0)\) \(m + 1\), or \(m\) itself

So homomorphic implies malleable implies not IND-CCA2. This is not a weakness of particular schemes; it is built into the goal. Computing on ciphertexts means producing new valid ciphertexts related to old ones, and that is precisely what the CCA2 adversary needs. (Non-malleability was formalized by Dolev, Dwork and Naor in 1991; Bellare, Desai, Pointcheval and Rogaway showed in 1998 that under adaptive chosen-ciphertext attack it is equivalent to IND-CCA2.) The argument assumes what every standard HE scheme provides: anyone holding the public keys can compute. Research variants that reserve computing for holders of a separate secret evaluation key (keyed-homomorphic encryption, Emura et al., 2013) can be CCA2 secure against everyone else.

Ordinary encryption reaches CCA2 by rejecting everything that honest encryption did not produce: encrypt-then-MAC, which tags the ciphertext with a message authentication code (MAC), and authenticated encryption with associated data (AEAD) (P3 §9), OAEP for RSA and the Fujisaki–Okamoto transform for ML-KEM (P4). The last two CCA2 rows in part 4 of the script show the effect. Hybrid encryption (P4 §6) without a MAC loses every game to P3's bit flip: flip a bit of the body, ask for the decryption, flip it back. With a hash-based message authentication code (HMAC) tag the modified ciphertext is rejected, and the adversary is back to a coin flip (49.8 %).

CCA1 and homomorphic encryption#

In the CCA1 game the oracle closes before the challenge arrives, so mauling \(c^*\) is impossible. The standard lattice HE schemes still fall, by key recovery. The attacker submits crafted ciphertexts that sit right on the edge between decrypting to 0 and decrypting to 1 (in P7's terms, their phase is near a decryption boundary). Which way each one falls depends on s, so each answer reveals a little about s, and enough answers reveal all of it. Loftus, May, Smart and Vercauteren did this to Gentry's scheme in 2011, Chenal and Tang (2014) extended such attacks to the later LWE- and RLWE-based schemes, and similar attacks apply to most lattice schemes (06 §1). CCA1 is not impossible in principle: there are theoretical CCA1-secure FHE constructions (Canetti, Raghuraman, Richelson and Vaikuntanathan, 2017), but the schemes used in practice do not have it. The practical rule: the key owner must never act as a decryption oracle for ciphertexts it did not produce or check.

8. What IND-CPA does not cover#

IND-CPA is the right target for HE, but it is a statement about one game. Everything outside that game is outside the guarantee:

Not covered Why it matters
Integrity IND-CPA is about reading, not changing. An HE server can compute the wrong function, or garbage, undetected (01 §5, 06 §1)
Chosen ciphertexts Impossible for HE (§7). The key owner must not decrypt untrusted ciphertexts
Side channels The game treats algorithms as black boxes. Real code leaks through timing and power: Kocher's 1996 timing attacks on RSA and Diffie–Hellman, RevEAL's single-trace attack on SEAL's noise sampler (2022) (06 §5)
Message length Equal lengths are part of the rules, so lengths may leak (CRIME)
Bad randomness The proofs assume fresh, uniform randomness. Reuse \(y\) in ElGamal and \(c_2 / c_2' = m / m'\) leaks; reuse a or e in LWE and differences of ciphertexts leak
Key-dependent messages The game's messages do not depend on sk. Bootstrapping keys encrypt s under itself, which needs circular security (P9)
Decrypted results The adversary never sees a decryption. In practice results get shared

The last row is the most important lesson of recent HE research. Li and Micciancio (2021) defined IND-CPA-D: the attacker may also see decryptions of honestly computed ciphertexts. For exact schemes with negligible decryption-failure probability, such as BFV, the Brakerski–Gentry–Vaikuntanathan scheme (BGV) and Torus fully homomorphic encryption (TFHE), those decryptions contain only the answer, which the attacker could predict, so IND-CPA implies IND-CPA-D. CKKS, however, is an approximate scheme: decryption does not round the noise away but returns the message plus the noise, \(m + e\), treating the noise as a small rounding error (P8 §9.3). Whoever holds the ciphertext \((a, b)\) and sees that unrounded output, the phase \(b - \langle a, s \rangle\), learns \(\langle a, s \rangle\): one shared decryption gives a linear equation in the secret key. A scheme with a valid IND-CPA proof falls to a passive key-recovery attack. The fix is noise flooding: add large fresh noise before revealing a decryption, so the original noise is drowned out. The fix and the later attacks on exact schemes are in 06 §3 and the IND-CPA-D deep dive, which reproduces the attack on the toy CKKS.

  • Advantages near 0 are sampling noise. With 2,000 games, \(\Pr[\text{win}]\) wanders about ±0.022 around 0.5 (two standard deviations of \(\sqrt{0.25 / 2000}\)), so 0.496, 0.501, 0.504 and 0.491 in part 3 are coin flips. Advantage 0.5 is the maximum: RSA, ECB and ElGamal in the wrong group.

10. How this connects to homomorphic encryption#

  • The HE server is the IND-CPA adversary. It holds the public key and the evaluation keys, sees every ciphertext and computes on them, but never sees a decryption. IND-CPA says it learns nothing about the data, which is exactly the promise of HE (ch. 01 §1).
  • Randomized encryption. IND-CPA forces it, and LWE provides it through the random mask a and the noise e: encrypting 0 twice gives two unrelated ciphertexts.
  • "Secure under RLWE" decoded. It is a reduction like §6: the pad \(\langle a, s \rangle + e\) looks uniform, so the ciphertext is a one-time pad (P1). The noise that makes this work is the same noise that grows with every operation (P7–P9).
  • Malleability is the price of computing. It was a bug in P3 and P4; here it is the reason HE can never be IND-CCA2. HE deployments compensate at the protocol level: the key owner does not decrypt untrusted ciphertexts, and integrity comes from elsewhere (proofs, replication, trusted execution environments (TEEs)).
  • Know the gaps. Circular security for bootstrapping (P9), and IND-CPA-D whenever decrypted results are shared (06 §3).

11. Check yourself#

  1. A developer makes AES-CTR "safer" by deriving the nonce from the message: nonce = SHA-256(m)[:12]. Is the result IND-CPA secure?
    Show answer

    No. Encryption is now deterministic: the same message always gets the same nonce and the same ciphertext. The adversary asks the oracle for \(\mathrm{Enc}(m_0)\) and compares it with \(c^*\), winning every time.

  2. Which of these are negligible: \(2^{-\lambda/2}\), \(\lambda^{-100}\), \(2^{-\log_2 \lambda}\), \(\lambda^{-\log \lambda}\)?
    Show answer

    \(2^{-\lambda/2}\) and \(\lambda^{-\log \lambda}\) are negligible: both eventually fall below every \(1/\lambda^c\). \(\lambda^{-100}\) is not (it never falls below \(1/\lambda^{101}\)), and \(2^{-\log_2 \lambda} = 1/\lambda\) is not either.

  3. An adversary wins the IND-CPA game with probability 0.2. What is its advantage, and should the scheme's designer worry?
    Show answer

    \(\lvert 0.2 - 0.5 \rvert = 0.3\). Yes: flipping its answers gives an adversary that wins with probability 0.8. Being reliably wrong is as informative as being reliably right.

  4. In the reduction from DDH to ElGamal, why does \(\mathcal{A}\) win with probability exactly \(\tfrac12\) when \(Z\) is random? What breaks if the group is all of \(\mathbb{Z}_p^*\)?
    Show answer

    \(Z\) is a uniform group element independent of \(b\), so \(m_b \cdot Z\) is uniform for either value of \(b\): a one-time pad. Nothing in \(\mathcal{A}\)'s view depends on \(b\). In all of \(\mathbb{Z}_p^*\) a real triple can be told from a random one with the Legendre symbol (DDH is easy), so the reduction would rest on a false assumption, and §4 shows an adversary that really wins.

  5. Paillier encryption (P6 §3) is IND-CPA secure (under the decisional composite residuosity assumption) and satisfies \(\mathrm{Enc}(a) \cdot \mathrm{Enc}(b) = \mathrm{Enc}(a + b)\). Show that it is not IND-CCA2 secure.
    Show answer

    Given \(c^*\), compute \(c' = c^* \cdot \mathrm{Enc}(1)\), which is a different ciphertext. The oracle returns \(m_b + 1\); subtract 1 and compare with \(m_0\). Advantage \(\tfrac12\). Any additively homomorphic scheme falls the same way.

  6. A CKKS-based analytics service publishes every decrypted result. The scheme is IND-CPA secure. Is the secret key safe?
    Show answer

    No. IND-CPA says nothing about shared decryptions. CKKS decryption returns the message plus the noise, and anyone who holds the ciphertext (the service computed it) can turn one published result into a linear equation for the secret key. That is the IND-CPA-D attack; the service needs noise flooding on decryption, or must stop publishing raw decryptions.

12. Key terms#

Term Meaning
Attacker model What the attacker may see and ask for (COA, KPA, CPA, CCA1, CCA2)
Encryption oracle A box that encrypts any message the attacker submits
Decryption oracle A box that decrypts any ciphertext the attacker submits (except the challenge)
Lunchtime attack Another name for CCA1: decryption access only before the challenge
Security parameter λ The knob that sizes keys and parameters; costs are measured as functions of it
PPT Probabilistic polynomial time: the class of "efficient" attackers
Negligible function Eventually smaller than \(1/p(\lambda)\) for every polynomial \(p\)
κ-bit security Every known attack costs about \(2^\kappa\) operations (time divided by success probability)
IND-CPA game The attacker picks \(m_0, m_1\), gets \(\mathrm{Enc}(m_b)\), and must guess \(b\)
Challenge ciphertext \(c^*\) The encryption of \(m_b\) that the attacker must classify
Advantage \(\lvert \Pr[b' = b] - \tfrac12 \rvert\): the edge over guessing
Randomized encryption Each message has many possible ciphertexts; required for IND-CPA
Semantic security Whatever is computable from the ciphertext is computable without it; equivalent to IND-CPA
Simulator The ciphertext-free algorithm in the semantic-security definition
Reduction An algorithm that uses any attacker on a scheme to solve a hard problem
Legendre symbol \(x^{(p-1)/2} \bmod p = \pm 1\): whether \(x\) is a square mod \(p\)
Decision-LWE Telling LWE samples \((a, \langle a, s \rangle + e)\) from uniform pairs
IND-CCA2 IND-CPA plus a decryption oracle for everything except \(c^*\)
Non-malleability Nobody can turn a ciphertext into one of a related message
Circular security Security even when the secret key is encrypted under itself
IND-CPA-D IND-CPA plus decryptions of honestly computed ciphertexts
Noise flooding Adding large fresh noise before revealing a decryption, so the original noise is drowned out

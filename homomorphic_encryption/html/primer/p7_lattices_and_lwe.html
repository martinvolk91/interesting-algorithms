P7 · Lattices and LWE: encryption from noisy equations#

← P6 Computing on ciphertexts · index · next: P8 Multiplication and relinearization →

Every practical FHE scheme rests on one problem, Learning With Errors (LWE), usually in its ring form. It starts from linear algebra you know: a system of linear equations mod \(q\) is easy to solve, but add a small random error to every equation and, as far as anyone knows, it becomes hard, even for quantum computers. Turned into encryption, LWE is a one-time pad whose pad is generated from a short key plus a little noise. Adding ciphertexts works out of the box. The noise is what makes it secure, and it is also what limits how much you can compute.

In this chapter

  • Solve \(b = A s \bmod q\) by elimination, and explain why the same method fails once noise is added.
  • State search-LWE and decision-LWE precisely, and say what the hardness results promise and what they do not.
  • Picture a lattice, say what makes lattice problems hard, and see LWE as one of them.
  • Encrypt and decrypt with LWE by hand, and draw the decryption sectors.
  • Add ciphertexts, multiply them by constants, and watch the noise budget run out.
  • Make the scheme public-key (Regev) and efficient (Ring-LWE).

Throughout, the toy parameters are \(n = 4\) (dimension), \(q = 97\) (a prime modulus), \(t = 4\) (plaintext modulus) and \(\Delta = \lfloor q/t \rfloor = 24\). The numbers are tiny so you can check every step on paper; ../code/toy_lwe.py is the same scheme with \(n = 16\), \(q = 2^{15}\), \(t = 16\).

1. Linear equations mod q are easy#

Pick a secret \(s \in \mathbb{Z}_q^n\). Publish random vectors \(a_i\) together with \(b_i = \langle a_i, s \rangle \bmod q\). With the script's secret \(s = (71, 1, 60, 83)\):

a = ( 66,  38,  69,  53)   b = 71
a = ( 90,  29,  12,  47)   b = 79
a = ( 79,   8,  43,  82)   b = 65
a = ( 32,  73,  26,   2)   b = 94

That is the system \(A s = b \pmod{97}\). Because 97 is prime, \(\mathbb{Z}_{97}\) is a field: every non-zero number has an inverse (for example \(66^{-1} = 25\), since \(66 \cdot 25 = 1650 = 17 \cdot 97 + 1\)). Gaussian elimination works exactly as over the reals, with division replaced by multiplication with the inverse. It returns \(s = (71, 1, 60, 83)\) after \(O(n^3)\) operations: under a billion for \(n = 1000\), about a second in compiled code.

Read \(\langle a_i, s \rangle\) as a pad derived from the key \(s\), the way a stream cipher derives a pad from a short key (P1 §7). Without noise these pads are linear in \(s\): after \(n\) of them the key is known and every later pad is predictable. It is the two-time pad again, in linear-algebra form.

2. Add a little noise: the LWE problem#

Now add a small random error to each equation:

\[b_i = \langle a_i, s \rangle + e_i \pmod q, \qquad e_i \text{ small}\]

In the toy, \(e_i\) is uniform in \(\{-3, \dots, 3\}\). Real schemes draw it from a discrete Gaussian with standard deviation about 3.2 (02 §2). That small change is the whole difference between an exercise and a foundation of modern cryptography.

Definition. Fix a dimension \(n\), a modulus \(q\) and an error distribution \(\chi\) that produces small integers. Choose \(s \in \mathbb{Z}_q^n\) uniformly. An LWE sample is

\[(a,\ \langle a, s \rangle + e \bmod q), \qquad a \in \mathbb{Z}_q^n \text{ uniform},\ e \leftarrow \chi\]

Stack \(\ell\) samples into a matrix \(A \in \mathbb{Z}_q^{\ell \times n}\) and a vector \(b = A s + e\) (we write \(\ell\) for the number of samples, because \(m\) will be the message). There are two versions of the problem:

  • Search-LWE: given \((A, b)\), find \(s\).
  • Decision-LWE: given \((A, b)\), decide whether \(b = A s + e\) or \(b\) is uniformly random in \(\mathbb{Z}_q^\ell\).

Decision looks easier, but for prime \(q\) of polynomial size Regev showed the two are equivalent. Decision-LWE is the version encryption needs, because IND-CPA (P5) asks that ciphertexts of any two chosen messages be indistinguishable, not merely that the key is hard to find. The proof gets there by showing that the pad looks random, which is exactly what decision-LWE says. With \(q = 2\), LWE is the older "learning parity with noise" problem; LWE generalizes it to larger moduli.

3. Why the noise defeats elimination#

Run the same elimination on noisy equations, \(b = A s + e\) with \(e = (3, 1, -2, -3)\). Since every step is linear, we can also apply each step to the noise column on its own and watch it (values shown as signed numbers between −48 and 48):

  pivot 1: divide by 66 = multiply by 66^-1 = 25 (mod 97);  noise column now (-22,  41, -10,  22)
  pivot 2: divide by 83 = multiply by 83^-1 = 90 (mod 97);  noise column now (-39,   4,  40, -17)
  pivot 3: divide by 80 = multiply by 80^-1 = 57 (mod 97);  noise column now (-21, -32, -48, -15)
  pivot 4: divide by 27 = multiply by 27^-1 = 18 (mod 97);  noise column now ( 19,  46,  36,  21)
elimination returns s' = ( 90,  47,  96,   7)   (the secret is ( 71,   1,  60,  83))

The very first pivot multiplies the first equation's noise by \(66^{-1} = 25\): \(3 \cdot 25 = 75 \equiv -22\). Every later step subtracts multiples of that row from the others, so the noise values get multiplied by large numbers and summed. At the end the answer is \(s' = s + A^{-1} e\), and \(A^{-1}\) is full of numbers spread over all of \(\mathbb{Z}_{97}\). Noise of size at most 3 has become \((19, 46, 36, 21)\): pure garbage.

Over the real numbers this would not happen. Least squares handles noisy equations routinely, because a small error times a moderate coefficient stays moderate. Mod \(q\) there is no notion of "small" that survives multiplication: \(3 \cdot 25 = 75\) wraps around to \(-22\).

More equations do not help elimination either. Any \(n\) of them give garbage. The script tests \(s'\) on four fresh samples: the residuals \(b - \langle a, s' \rangle\) are \(-6, 47, -1, -27\), while the true \(s\) gives \(2, -2, -2, -2\). A wrong guess sometimes fits one equation by luck (the \(-1\)); it does not fit all of them.

What does work on a toy is brute force: try all \(q^n = 97^4 \approx 8.9 \cdot 10^7\) candidates and keep the one whose residuals are all small. The cost \(q^n\) is exponential in \(n\). The best known attacks are much cleverer than brute force, but for well-chosen parameters they are still exponential in \(n\). They are lattice attacks.

4. Lattices in pictures#

A lattice is the set of all integer combinations of some basis vectors:

\[\mathcal{L}(B) = \{\, z_1 b_1 + \dots + z_n b_n : z_i \in \mathbb{Z} \,\}\]

Here is a two-dimensional lattice. The points are the lattice; O is the origin.

    .         .         .         .         .
        .         .         .         .         .
  .         .         D         .         .
      .         .         .         .         .
.         .         .         .         .         .
    .         C         .         .         .
        .         .         .         .         .
  B         .         .         .         .
      A         .         .         .         .
O         .         .         .         .         .

The vectors \(OA = (3, 1)\) and \(OB = (1, 2)\) form a basis. So do \(OC = (7, 4)\) and \(OD = (11, 7)\), because \(OC = 2 \cdot OA + OB\) and \(OD = 3 \cdot OA + 2 \cdot OB\) with \(\det \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = 1\). Both generate exactly the same points. But \(\{A, B\}\) is a good basis: short vectors (lengths 3.2 and 2.2) at 45° to each other. \(\{C, D\}\) is a bad basis: long vectors (8.1 and 13.0), only 2.7° apart.

The central hard problems (02 §1):

  • SVP (shortest vector problem): find the shortest non-zero lattice vector.
  • CVP (closest vector problem): given a point that is not on the lattice, find the nearest lattice point. BDD (bounded distance decoding) is the special case where the point is promised to be very close to the lattice.

In two dimensions you solve both by looking. With a good basis, BDD is easy in any dimension: write the target in basis coordinates and round them (Babai's method). If the target is close enough to the lattice, the rounded coordinates give the nearest point. With a bad basis the rounded coordinates land far away. In dimension 500 or 1000, given only a bad basis, nobody knows an efficient way.

LWE is a lattice problem. The vectors \(A z \bmod q\), for all \(z \in \mathbb{Z}_q^n\), together with all multiples of \(q\) in each coordinate, form a lattice in \(\mathbb{Z}^\ell\) (a "\(q\)-ary" lattice). \(A s\) is one of its points, and \(b = A s + e\) is a point at distance \(\lVert e \rVert\) from it. Finding \(s\) is a BDD problem, and the only basis an attacker can write down comes from the random matrix \(A\): a bad one.

What matters is noise relative to \(q\). Dividing everything by \(q\), the equations hold up to an error of size about \(\lvert e \rvert / q\). With the noise fixed, a bigger \(q\) makes the equations look almost exact, the point \(b\) is relatively closer to the lattice, and lattice reduction (below) succeeds more easily. So a scheme that needs a big \(q\), for example to survive many multiplications (P8), must also use a bigger dimension to stay secure. That trade-off is the parameter table of 02 §6.

The attack family: lattice reduction. Algorithms that turn a bad basis into a better one are the main tool against LWE. LLL (Lenstra, Lenstra, Lovász, 1982) runs in polynomial time but only guarantees a vector within a factor \(2^{(n-1)/2}\) of the shortest, which is useless for cryptographic dimensions. BKZ (Schnorr 1987; Schnorr and Euchner 1994) works on blocks of \(\beta\) coordinates at a time and gets closer as \(\beta\) grows, at a cost exponential in \(\beta\). Breaking a parameter set means running BKZ with a large enough block size. The best known methods for that inner step (lattice sieving) take roughly \(2^{0.29\beta}\) operations heuristically, and known quantum speedups only lower the constant in the exponent a little. Security estimates for FHE parameters are exactly this calculation (02 §6, 06 §2).

Worst-case hardness, in one paragraph#

Most cryptography assumes that random instances of a problem are hard, because that is what a random key produces. Regev (2005) proved something stronger for LWE: if an efficient algorithm solves LWE on random instances (with noise above a threshold that grows like \(\sqrt{n}\)), then there is an efficient quantum algorithm that approximately solves the lattice problems GapSVP and SIVP on every lattice of dimension \(n\), to within factors polynomial in \(n\). (Both are approximate versions of SVP: GapSVP asks to decide roughly how short the shortest vector is, SIVP asks for \(n\) short linearly independent vectors.) So breaking random LWE would break the hardest instances of problems studied for decades. Peikert (2009) gave a classical, non-quantum version of the reduction for exponentially large moduli \(q \ge 2^{n/2}\), and Brakerski, Langlois, Peikert, Regev and Stehlé (2013) extended the classical result to polynomial moduli (from lattices of somewhat smaller dimension). Two caveats keep this honest. The results are asymptotic and say nothing about a concrete \(n\). And deployed FHE parameters use noise (\(\sigma \approx 3.2\) at every dimension) smaller than the theorem's threshold, so their security rests on the attack estimates above, not on the theorem.

Why quantum computers do not help (as far as anyone knows)#

Shor's algorithm breaks factoring and discrete logarithms by finding a hidden period. No comparable structure is known in lattice problems. No known quantum algorithm solves LWE substantially faster than classical ones; a 2024 claim of one was withdrawn within about ten days, after a bug was found (02 §7). That is why NIST's post-quantum key-encapsulation standard ML-KEM (FIPS 203, P4) is built on a variant of LWE, and why FHE is post-quantum too.

5. Building encryption: the noisy one-time pad#

The one-time pad mod \(q\) (P1 §4) is \(c = m + k\) with a truly random pad \(k\). LWE encryption keeps that shape and changes two things:

  1. The pad is \(\langle a, s \rangle + e\): generated from the short key \(s\) and a public random \(a\), plus a little noise so that it looks random.
  2. The message is scaled into the top of \(\mathbb{Z}_q\), as \(\Delta \cdot m\) with \(\Delta = \lfloor q / t \rfloor\), so that the noise cannot reach it.
\[b = \underbrace{\langle a, s \rangle + e}_{\text{noisy pad}} + \Delta \cdot m \pmod q, \qquad \text{ciphertext} = (a, b)\]

Decryption subtracts the pad, which the key holder can recompute except for the noise. What is left is the phase:

\[\varphi = b - \langle a, s \rangle = \Delta \cdot m + e \pmod q, \qquad m = \mathrm{round}(\varphi / \Delta) \bmod t\]

Rounding removes \(e\). It works as long as \(\lvert e \rvert < \Delta / 2\). When \(t\) divides \(q\), \(\Delta = q/t\) exactly, and dividing by \(\Delta\) is the same as multiplying by \(t/q\): that is the form chapter 03 uses ("scale by \(t/q\) and round").

Security is the one-time-pad argument with a pseudo-random pad. Decision-LWE says \((a, \langle a, s \rangle + e)\) looks uniformly random. Adding \(\Delta \cdot m\) to a uniform value gives a uniform value. So \((a, b)\) looks uniform whatever \(m\) is, which is IND-CPA under the decision-LWE assumption. Every encryption uses a fresh \(a\) and \(e\), so encrypting the same message twice gives unrelated ciphertexts.

A hand-worked example#

Secret \(s = (71, 1, 60, 83)\). Encrypt \(m = 1\) with mask \(a = (85, 8, 27, 67)\) and noise \(e = +2\) (the script fixes \(e\) here so the example is easy to follow; normally it is random):

Step Computation Value
Pad \(85 \cdot 71 + 8 \cdot 1 + 27 \cdot 60 + 67 \cdot 83 = 6035 + 8 + 1620 + 5561 = 13224\) \(13224 = 136 \cdot 97 + 32\), so 32
Scaled message \(\Delta \cdot m = 24 \cdot 1\) 24
\(b\) \(32 + 24 + 2\) 58
Ciphertext \((a, b)\) \(((85, 8, 27, 67),\ 58)\)
Phase \(58 - 32\) 26
Decode \(26 / 24 = 1.08\), round 1

A second one shows the wrap-around. Encrypt \(m = 0\) with \(a = (86, 23, 24, 22)\) and \(e = -2\). The pad is \(86 \cdot 71 + 23 + 24 \cdot 60 + 22 \cdot 83 = 9395 \equiv 83\), so \(b = 83 + 0 - 2 = 81\). Decrypting gives phase \(81 - 83 = -2 \equiv 95\), and \(95 / 24 = 3.96\) rounds to 4, which is \(0 \bmod 4\). Correct, because 95 sits just below 0 on the circle.

The circle picture#

Arrange \(\mathbb{Z}_{97}\) on a circle (Regev's paper draws it the same way). The message picks one of \(t = 4\) sectors, each centred on \(\Delta \cdot m\). The noise is the offset from the centre. Decryption asks which sector the phase landed in.

                                 0
                      84  \               /  12
                           \  *95        /
                            \   m = 0   /
                             \         /
                              \       /
          72        m = 3        ( )        m = 1        24
                              /       \           * 26
                             /         \
                            /   m = 2   \
                           /             \
                      60  /               \  36
                                 48

The two example phases are marked: 26 lies 2 past the centre of sector 1, and 95 lies 2 before the centre of sector 0. Unrolled, the sectors are:

Phase \(\varphi\) (mod 97) 84 … 96 and 0 … 11 12 … 35 36 … 59 60 … 83
Decrypts to 0 1 2 3
Sector centre \(\Delta \cdot m\) 0 24 48 72

(Sector 0 has 25 values because \(97 = 4 \cdot 24 + 1\).) Decryption is correct while the offset stays within \(\pm 11\) of the centre, that is \(\lvert e \rvert < \Delta / 2 = 12\). One step further and the phase is in the neighbouring sector: the message is not slightly wrong, it is a different message.

6. Homomorphic addition and the noise budget#

Add two ciphertexts componentwise:

\[(a_1, b_1) + (a_2, b_2) = (a_1 + a_2,\ b_1 + b_2)\]

Everything adds: the pads, because \(\langle a_1, s \rangle + \langle a_2, s \rangle = \langle a_1 + a_2, s \rangle\), the messages and the noises. The sum is a valid ciphertext of \(m_1 + m_2 \bmod t\) with noise \(e_1 + e_2\). In the script, \(\mathrm{Enc}(1)\) with noise \(+2\) plus \(\mathrm{Enc}(2)\) with noise \(-1\) has phase \(73 = 24 \cdot 3 + 1\), and decrypts to 3.

Plaintext times ciphertext. Multiplying a ciphertext by a public integer \(k\) multiplies everything by \(k\): \(k \cdot (a, b)\) has phase \(k \Delta m + k e\). The message is multiplied, and so is the noise. With the noise-\((+2)\) encryption of 1, the script gets noise \(+4\) for \(k = 2\) and \(+6\) for \(k = 3\). For \(k = 4\) it gets \(+7\), not \(+8\): the message \(4\) wraps to \(0\), and since \(4 \Delta = 96 = q - 1\), each wrap past \(t\) leaves an extra \(-1\) (in general \(-(q \bmod t)\)). It is a quirk of choosing \(q\) not divisible by \(t\), and with real moduli of hundreds of bits it is negligible. An encryption of 0 with noise \(+2\), multiplied by 5, still decrypts (noise 10). Multiplied by 6 it has noise 12, the phase lands on the sector boundary, and it decrypts to 1.

The noise budget. The gap between the current noise and \(\Delta / 2\) is the noise budget: every operation spends some of it, and when it is gone the message is destroyed. This chapter measures it linearly, as a margin \(\Delta/2 - \lvert e \rvert\) (10 for noise \(\pm 2\) here). From P8 on, and in libraries, it is measured in bits, \(\log_2\big((\Delta/2)/\lvert e \rvert\big)\) (here \(\log_2 6 \approx 2.6\) bits), because multiplications multiply the noise and a multiplication then costs a fixed number of bits. The script starts from \(\mathrm{Enc}(1)\) and keeps adding fresh encryptions of 0, so the message never changes and only the noise moves (each fresh \(e\) is in \([-3, 3]\); output abridged):

  added | decrypts | noise | -12         0         +12
      0 |    1     |   +2  |             |##
      1 |    1     |   +3  |             |###
      ...
     13 |    1     |   +3  |             |###
     14 |    1     |   +5  |             |#####
     15 |    1     |   +6  |             |######
     ...
     20 |    1     |   +7  |             |#######
     21 |    1     |  +10  |             |##########
     22 |    2     |  +12  |             |############  <- decryption FAILED

The noise does a random walk. In the worst case it grows linearly with the number of additions (\(3k\) after \(k\)), on average more like \(\sqrt{k}\) because positive and negative errors partly cancel. Here it took 22 additions. Real parameters have \(\Delta\) astronomically larger than the noise, so additions are almost free; it is multiplication that eats the budget fast (P8), and bootstrapping that refills it (P9).

7. Public-key encryption: Regev's trick#

So far only the holder of \(s\) can encrypt. Regev's 2005 paper made it public-key with a simple idea: publish many encryptions of 0.

  • Public key: \(\ell\) samples \((a_i, b_i = \langle a_i, s \rangle + e_i)\), each an encryption of 0. The toy uses \(\ell = 8\) with \(e_i \in \{-1, 0, 1\}\).
  • Encrypt \(m\): choose a random subset \(S\) of the samples, add them up, and add \(\Delta \cdot m\) to the \(b\) part:
\[\mathrm{Enc}(m) = \Big(\sum_{i \in S} a_i,\ \ \sum_{i \in S} b_i + \Delta \cdot m\Big)\]
  • Decrypt exactly as before.

It works because a sum of encryptions of 0 is an encryption of 0 (§6), and adding \(\Delta \cdot m\) turns it into an encryption of \(m\). Its noise is \(\sum_{i \in S} e_i\), at most \(\ell\) times the public key's noise. That is why the toy's public key uses smaller noise than fresh secret-key encryptions: \(8 \cdot 1 = 8 < 12\).

From the script: \(\mathrm{Enc}(1)\) used the subset \(\{0, 1, 4, 6\}\), giving \(((47, 75, 15, 5), 0)\) with noise \(+2\), which decrypts to 1.

Why it is secure. Two steps. By decision-LWE, the public key looks like \(\ell\) uniformly random pairs. And for uniformly random pairs, the sum over a random subset of \(\ell \ge (1 + \varepsilon)(n + 1)\log_2 q\) of them is statistically close to uniform (a special case of the leftover hash lemma, proved in Regev's paper), so it hides \(\Delta \cdot m\). The toy's \(\ell = 8\) is far too small: there are only \(2^8\) subsets to try. Regev's original scheme encrypts single bits with \(\lfloor q/2 \rfloor\) as the scale; the public key has about \(n^2 \log q\) numbers, which is what the ring version fixes.

Chapter 03 §2 uses the ring version of the same idea: its public key is one encryption of 0, and encryption multiplies it by a small random polynomial u instead of picking a subset. It also adds fresh small noises e₁ and e₂ to the two components of the ciphertext. That is why chapter 03's decryption gives \(\Delta m + e\,u + e_1 + e_2\, s\): the public key's noise e times u (the counterpart of \(\sum_{i \in S} e_i\) here), plus the two fresh noises. (Chapter 03 writes ciphertexts in the \((c_0, c_1)\) form that P8 §1 introduces.)

8. Ring-LWE: polynomials instead of vectors#

Plain LWE is bulky. One message mod \(t\) costs a ciphertext of \(n + 1\) numbers, and a public matrix \(A\) has \(n^2\) entries. Ring-LWE (Lyubashevsky, Peikert, Regev, 2010) replaces the vectors by polynomials in

\[R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[X] / (X^N + 1)\]

the polynomials of degree less than \(N\) with coefficients mod \(q\), multiplied with the rule \(X^N = -1\).

The negacyclic wrap-around#

Multiplying by \(X\) shifts every coefficient up one place, and the top coefficient wraps around to the constant term with a minus sign. In \(\mathbb{Z}_{97}[X]/(X^4 + 1)\):

\[X \cdot (5 + X + 7X^2 + 2X^3) = 5X + X^2 + 7X^3 + 2X^4 = -2 + 5X + X^2 + 7X^3\]

Chapter 02's example comes out the same: \((1 + 2X + X^3)(3 + X^2) = 3 + 5X + X^2 + 5X^3\) (there, \(X^5 = -X\) moves a term from the top back to \(X\)).

Why \(X^N + 1\) and not the plain cyclic \(X^N - 1\)? \(X^N - 1\) has the root \(X = 1\), and evaluating at 1 keeps small things small: each sample would yield a one-dimensional equation \(b(1) = a(1) s(1) + e(1)\) whose unknowns are sums of small coefficients, which leaks information about \(s\). \(X^N + 1\), for \(N\) a power of two, is the \(2N\)-th cyclotomic polynomial (its roots are the \(N\) primitive \(2N\)-th roots of unity). It is irreducible over the integers, and no comparable shortcut is known for it. The hardness proof of Lyubashevsky, Peikert and Regev is for cyclotomic rings like this one.

One RLWE sample is N LWE samples#

Multiplication by a fixed polynomial \(a\) is a linear map, so \(a \cdot s = M_a s\) for an \(N \times N\) matrix whose column \(j\) holds the coefficients of \(a \cdot X^j\). For the script's mask \(a = 86 + 3X + 59X^2 + 21X^3\) the first row of \(M_a\) is \((86, 76, 38, 94)\) and the second is \((3, 86, 76, 38)\); the whole matrix is in the output of §9. Each column is the previous one shifted down by one, with the entry pushed out at the bottom coming back on top negated (\(-21 \equiv 76\), \(-59 \equiv 38\), \(-3 \equiv 94\)). This is a negacyclic matrix. An RLWE sample \((a, b = a \cdot s + e)\) is therefore \(N\) ordinary LWE samples whose vectors are the rows of \(M_a\), all sharing the secret \(s\).

That is where the efficiency comes from:

  • Size. Storing \(a\) takes \(N\) numbers instead of the \(N^2\) of a random matrix. A ciphertext \((a, b)\) of \(2N\) numbers carries \(N\) message coefficients, where LWE needs \(n + 1\) numbers per message: roughly \(N/2\) times less data. The ratio of ciphertext size to message size is the ciphertext expansion: \(2N \log_2 q\) bits carry \(N \log_2 t\) bits, a factor \(2 \log_2 q / \log_2 t\) (about 55 with chapter 02's \(\log_2 q = 438\) and a 16-bit \(t\)).
  • Speed. \(a \cdot s\) costs \(O(N \log N)\) with the Number Theoretic Transform (NTT), an FFT over \(\mathbb{Z}_q\) that needs \(q \equiv 1 \pmod{2N}\). A matrix-vector product costs \(O(N^2)\). Chapter 02 explains the NTT (02 §4); it is the kernel that FHE hardware accelerates.

The NTT in miniature, mod 17 as in chapter 02 (\(17 \equiv 1 \pmod 8\)): the roots of \(X^4 + 1\) mod 17 are \(2, 8, 15, 9\), that is \(2, 2^3, 2^5, 2^7\) (and \(2^4 = 16 \equiv -1\)). Evaluating at these four roots turns a polynomial into four numbers: \(1 + 2X + X^3 \mapsto (13, 2, 6, 0)\) and \(3 + X^2 \mapsto (7, 16, 7, 16)\). Multiplying pointwise gives \((6, 15, 8, 0)\), which is exactly the evaluation of their negacyclic product \(3 + 5X + X^2 + 5X^3\) from above. Each root \(r\) satisfies \(r^4 = -1\), so evaluation respects the rule \(X^4 = -1\), and one ring product costs \(N\) multiplications of numbers. The NTT computes the evaluations, and the way back, in \(O(N \log N)\).

The price is extra algebraic structure an attacker might exploit. Lyubashevsky, Peikert and Regev proved decision-RLWE hard assuming worst-case problems on ideal lattices are hard for quantum algorithms. No attack is known that does better on the power-of-two cyclotomic rings used in FHE than on plain LWE (02 §3). ML-KEM sits in between: Module-LWE, small matrices of ring elements with \(N = 256\).

An RLWE encryption#

Encryption and decryption look exactly like §5 with polynomials in place of vectors: \(b = a \cdot s + \Delta m + e\), and the phase \(b - a \cdot s\) is rounded coefficient by coefficient. In practice the secret is small (coefficients in \(\{-1, 0, 1\}\)), as here. The script uses \(s = X - X^2 + X^3\), noise \(e = (-3, 3, -2, -1)\) and the message polynomial \(m = (3, 1, 0, 2)\) (coefficients of \(1, X, X^2, X^3\)):

Coefficients
\(a \cdot s\) \((35, 48, 90, 45)\)
\(\Delta \cdot m\) \((72, 24, 0, 48)\)
\(b = a \cdot s + \Delta m + e\) \((7, 75, 88, 92)\)
Phase \(b - a \cdot s\) \((69, 27, 95, 47)\)
\(\div 24\), round, mod 4 \((3, 1, 0, 2)\)

Addition works coefficient by coefficient, exactly as in §6. Multiplying by a public polynomial \(k\) (plaintext times ciphertext) turns the noise into \(k \cdot e\). Each coefficient of \(k \cdot e\) is a signed sum of products \(k_i e_j\), so the noise grows by a factor of at most \(\lVert k \rVert_1 = \sum_i \lvert k_i \rvert\); chapter 03 §3 says "about \(\lVert k \rVert\)".

Packing N values#

The message here is a polynomial with \(N\) coefficients, so one ciphertext holds \(N\) numbers. Adding ciphertexts adds them coefficient by coefficient, but multiplying multiplies polynomials, which mixes coefficients. Batching fixes that: if the plaintext modulus \(t\) is a prime with \(t \equiv 1 \pmod{2N}\), the Chinese Remainder Theorem splits \(\mathbb{Z}_t[X]/(X^N+1)\) into \(N\) independent "slots", and one homomorphic multiplication multiplies all \(N\) slots pairwise, like a SIMD instruction (05 §1). With \(N = 16384\) that is 16384 multiplications for the price of one.

10. How this connects to homomorphic encryption#

  • The noisy one-time pad. An LWE ciphertext is a one-time pad whose pad \(\langle a, s \rangle\) is generated from a short key, plus a little noise. The noise makes the pad look random (that is the security), and the same noise grows with every operation (that is the limit).
  • Addition is free; the noise budget is not. Pads, messages and noises all add. Every FHE scheme manages this budget; 03 §1 runs the same scheme with bigger numbers.
  • The form of a ciphertext. In this chapter a ciphertext is \((a, b)\) with \(b = \langle a, s \rangle + \Delta m + e\). P8 §1 writes the same thing as \((c_0, c_1) = (b, -a)\), so that decryption reads \(c_0 + c_1 \cdot s = \Delta m + e\), the form used in chapter 03, and then multiplies ciphertexts.
  • Decryption is an inner product plus a rounding. That small function is what bootstrapping (P9) evaluates homomorphically to reset the noise.

11. Check yourself#

  1. With the chapter's key \(s = (71, 1, 60, 83)\), \(q = 97\), \(t = 4\), decrypt the ciphertext \(((1, 0, 0, 0),\ 2)\). What is its noise?
  2. Why does Gaussian elimination cope with noisy equations over the real numbers but not mod \(q\)?
  3. Two messages are encrypted under the same key with the same mask \(a\). What can an eavesdropper learn, and which P1 attack is this?
  4. With \(\Delta / 2 = 12\) and fresh noise \(\lvert e \rvert \le 3\), how many fresh ciphertexts can you add with a guarantee of correct decryption (messages that do not wrap past \(t\))? Why do more additions usually work in practice?
  5. In Regev's public-key scheme with \(\ell = 8\) public samples, how large may the public key's noise be if decryption must always succeed with \(\Delta / 2 = 12\)?
  6. Compute \(X^2 \cdot (1 + 2X + 3X^2)\) in \(\mathbb{Z}_q[X]/(X^4 + 1)\).

Answers

  1. \(\langle a, s \rangle = 71\), so the phase is \(2 - 71 = -69 \equiv 28\). \(28 / 24 = 1.17\) rounds to 1: the message is 1, with noise \(28 - 24 = +4\).
  2. Over the reals, multiplying a small error by a moderate coefficient keeps it moderate, and least squares averages errors out. Mod \(q\), multiplying by an inverse like \(25\) and reducing wraps a small error to an arbitrary-looking residue, so after elimination the error \(A^{-1} e\) is spread over all of \(\mathbb{Z}_q\).
  3. \(b_1 - b_2 = \Delta (m_1 - m_2) + (e_1 - e_2)\): the pads cancel, and rounding \((b_1 - b_2)/\Delta\) reveals \(m_1 - m_2\). It is the two-time pad.
  4. Three: \(\lvert e_1 + e_2 + e_3 \rvert \le 9 < 12\), but four could reach exactly 12. In practice the errors are random and partly cancel, so the sum grows like \(\sqrt{k}\) rather than \(3k\); the script's run lasted 22 additions.
  5. A subset can contain all 8 samples, so \(8 B < 12\): the public key's noise must satisfy \(\lvert e_i \rvert \le 1\).
  6. \(X^2 + 2X^3 + 3X^4 = -3 + X^2 + 2X^3\), because \(X^4 = -1\).

12. Key terms#

Term Meaning
LWE sample \((a, \langle a, s \rangle + e \bmod q)\) with uniform \(a\) and small error \(e\)
Search-LWE Find \(s\) from many LWE samples
Decision-LWE Tell LWE samples apart from uniformly random pairs; what encryption security needs
Error / noise The small random \(e\) added to each equation or ciphertext
Lattice All integer combinations of a set of basis vectors
Good / bad basis Short, nearly orthogonal vectors versus long, nearly parallel ones for the same lattice
SVP, CVP, BDD Find a shortest vector; the closest lattice point; the closest point when it is very near
GapSVP, SIVP Approximate SVP variants: estimate the shortest length; find \(n\) short independent vectors
\(q\)-ary lattice The lattice of vectors congruent mod \(q\) to some \(A z\); LWE is BDD in it
Lattice reduction (LLL, BKZ) Algorithms that improve a basis; the main attack on LWE
Worst-case to average-case reduction A proof that solving random LWE instances would (approximately) solve every instance of a lattice problem
Scaling factor \(\Delta\) \(\lfloor q / t \rfloor\): puts the message in the top of \(\mathbb{Z}_q\), away from the noise
Phase \(b - \langle a, s \rangle = \Delta m + e\), what decryption rounds
Noise budget How much more noise a ciphertext can take before \(\lvert e \rvert\) reaches \(\Delta / 2\): a margin here, bits from P8 on
Regev encryption Public-key LWE: publish encryptions of 0, encrypt with a random subset sum
Ring-LWE LWE with polynomials in \(\mathbb{Z}_q[X]/(X^N + 1)\) instead of vectors
Negacyclic Multiplication mod \(X^N + 1\): coefficients wrap around with a sign flip
NTT FFT over \(\mathbb{Z}_q\); multiplies ring elements in \(O(N \log N)\)
Ciphertext expansion Ciphertext size divided by message size
Batching Packing \(N\) values into slots of one ciphertext via the CRT
Module-LWE Small matrices of ring elements; the basis of ML-KEM

Deep dive: FHE hardware (why it's slow, and how GPUs and ASICs fix it)#

index · background: 02 §4 NTT, 02 §5 RNS, 03 §5 key switching, 07 §4 hardware

Every deep dive so far ran into the same wall: FHE is slow. This one asks why, at the level of arithmetic and memory, and looks at how CPUs, GPUs, FPGAs and dedicated chips attack the problem, from academic designs (F1, BTS, CraterLake, ARK, SHARP) to Intel's fabricated Heracles.

Measurements come from ../code/hw_profile.py, which profiles the earlier toys, implements a real negacyclic NTT, demonstrates RNS limbs, and runs a cost model of key switching at bootstrapping-size parameters:

python3 code/hw_profile.py       # from the homomorphic_encryption/ folder, ~2 s

Python is useless for absolute speed. What it shows reliably is where the work goes and how it scales.

0. The one idea#

FHE turns one plaintext operation into millions of modular multiplications on huge polynomials, plus megabytes to gigabytes of key material streamed through the chip. Three things dominate:

  1. Polynomial multiplication, done with the NTT: lots of compute, but it parallelizes well.
  2. Key switching (relinearization, rotations, bootstrapping): NTTs plus basis conversion plus reading a large key.
  3. Memory bandwidth: the keys don't fit in cache, so the chip waits for them.

Accelerators win with wide parallel NTT/modular-multiply lanes, huge on-chip memory, and above all by generating or reusing data instead of loading it.

1. Where the time goes#

1.1 In our toys#

Profiling each toy's most expensive operation, as a share of run time by function:

CKKS bootstrapping (toy_ckks)        0.06 s   poly_mul 71%, centre 19%, <lambda> 4%, <genexpr> 1%
TFHE programmable bootstrap (toy)    0.17 s   poly_mul 95%, decompose 2%, poly_add 1%, external_product 1%
BFV multiply + rotate (toy_bfv)      0.04 s   poly_mul_int 93%, centre 4%, key_switch 1%, poly_add 1%

Ring multiplication is 90–95 % of everything (poly_mul plus centre, its modular reduction, in the CKKS toy). The rest (decomposition, automorphisms, additions) is noise. The toys use schoolbook \(O(N^2)\) multiplication, so this is the worst case. With an NTT the share drops, and data movement and basis conversion move to the front.

1.2 In real CKKS bootstrapping#

The ARK paper measured the computational workload of bootstrapping at practical parameters: NTT 54.8 %, basis conversion 34.2 %, everything else ~11 % (as summarized by the FHE accelerator SoK, arXiv 2212.01713). Recent CKKS bootstrapping implementations spend 55–72 % of their time in the CoeffToSlot/SlotToCoeff linear transforms, which are rotations, i.e. key switches (CKKS deep dive §5.7).

2. The NTT: from O(N²) to O(N log N)#

2.1 What it computes#

The negacyclic NTT evaluates a polynomial at the N odd powers of a 2N-th root of unity ψ mod a prime p (02 §4). In that "evaluation domain", ring multiplication is pointwise:

\[a \cdot b = \mathrm{INTT}\big(\mathrm{NTT}(a) \odot \mathrm{NTT}(b)\big)\]

hw_profile.py implements it the standard way. Cooley–Tukey butterflies go forward and Gentleman–Sande butterflies go back, with the multiplication by powers of ψ (the "negacyclic twist") folded into the twiddle factors:

def forward(self, a):
    a, p, n = a[:], self.p, self.n
    t, m = n, 1
    while m < n:                                         # log2(n) stages of n/2 butterflies
        t //= 2
        for i in range(m):
            w = self.psi_rev[m + i]
            for j in range(2 * i * t, 2 * i * t + t):
                u, v = a[j], a[j + t] * w % p
                a[j], a[j + t] = (u + v) % p, (u - v) % p
        m *= 2
    return a

Each butterfly is one modular multiplication, one addition and one subtraction, and there are (N/2)·log₂N of them per transform:

 stage 1 (stride N/2)      stage 2 (stride N/4)      …   stage log N (stride 1)
 a0 ──┬── a0+w·a4          a0 ──┬──                        neighbours
 a4 ──┴── a0−w·a4          a2 ──┴──

2.2 Measured#

Multiplying two random polynomials mod a 30-bit NTT-friendly prime. Both methods give identical results (the script asserts it):

    64        0.3 ms      0.1 ms       3x                  4,096        704
   128        1.1 ms      0.2 ms       5x                 16,384      1,600
   256        4.6 ms      0.5 ms      10x                 65,536      3,584
   512       27.9 ms      1.4 ms      20x                262,144      7,936
  1024       89.9 ms      3.5 ms      25x              1,048,576     17,408
  2048      378.6 ms      6.0 ms      63x              4,194,304     37,888

At bootstrapping size (N = 2¹⁶), schoolbook needs about \(4.3 \times 10^9\) modular multiplications per product, while the NTT route needs about \(1.7 \times 10^6\): ~2 500× fewer. No real FHE library multiplies polynomials any other way.

2.3 What makes the NTT hard for hardware#

  • Data access, not arithmetic. Each stage pairs elements at a different stride (N/2, N/4, …, 1). A vector or SIMD unit needs shuffles between stages, and an ASIC needs a transpose network. The standard fix is the 4-step (Bailey) NTT: view the N points as a matrix, do small NTTs on the columns, multiply by twiddles, transpose, and do small NTTs on the rows. F1 builds a 2¹⁴-point NTT from 128-wide vector operations this way. BTS splits a 2¹⁷-point NTT into 64 × 64 × 32 steps across a 2-D array of processing elements.
  • Twiddle factors per limb. Each RNS prime (§3) needs its own table of ψ powers, which is extra memory that FFTs over ℂ don't need (SoK §4.1.2).
  • Modular multiplication of 30–64-bit words (Barrett or Montgomery reduction) costs several integer multiplies. Multiplier area grows roughly with the square of the word width, which is why designs trend toward shorter words: SHARP uses 36-bit words, and the Cheddar GPU library uses 32-bit.
  • Sequential depth. A TFHE blind rotation is n external products in sequence, each containing NTTs. Latency, not throughput, is the limit. That is why the CRYPTO 2026 result cutting the sequential NTT layers from 500+ to 3 is aimed squarely at parallel hardware (07 §1).

3. RNS: why hardware works on 30–64-bit words#

Ciphertext moduli are 500–1 700 bits. Instead of big-integer arithmetic, the modulus is a product of word-size NTT-friendly primes, and every polynomial is stored as one residue polynomial ("limb") per prime (02 §5). Measured:

N = 1024, Q = product of 20 NTT-friendly 30-bit primes (600 bits)
big-integer schoolbook mod Q:   0.43 s
RNS: 20 independent 30-bit NTT multiplications (+ CRT check):   0.12 s   identical result: True

In Python the gain is modest. For hardware, it's everything: the 600-bit product became 20 completely independent word-size NTT multiplications, which can go to 20 parallel lanes with no carries between them. Accelerators organize their parallelism around two axes:

Axis What runs in parallel Good for Problem
limb-level (rPLP in the SoK) different RNS limbs on different units NTT, element-wise ops basis conversion mixes all limbs
coefficient-level (CLP) slices of the same polynomial on different units basis conversion NTT needs cross-unit transposes

Basis conversion (BConv) is the step that breaks limb independence. It re-expresses a polynomial from one set of primes in another, which needs every limb's coefficient k to produce every output limb's coefficient k: an all-to-all mixing of limbs. Key switching (§4) and rescaling need it. CraterLake gave it a dedicated unit (the CRB unit, 34 % of the chip area per the SoK), and ARK switches between limb-level and coefficient-level distribution depending on the operation.

4. Key switching: the operation that sets the speed limit#

4.1 Anatomy of hybrid key switching#

Every relinearization and every rotation is a key switch (03 §5). The variant everyone uses splits the L limbs into dnum digits of α limbs each, with a temporary special modulus of k = α limbs:

 input polynomial (L limbs, NTT form)
   │  split into dnum digits of α limbs
   ▼
 ModUp      per digit: INTT α limbs → BConv to the other L−α+k limbs → NTT them
   ▼
 inner product with the key: Σ_digits digit × evk_digit  (2 polys × dnum × (L+k) limbs)  ← READ THE KEY
   ▼
 ModDown    INTT k limbs → BConv back to L limbs → NTT → subtract, × P⁻¹
   ▼
 2 output polynomials (L limbs)

4.2 A cost model at bootstrapping size#

hw_profile.py counts the operations of one key switch at N = 2¹⁶ and L = 24 limbs of 64 bits. It then times them on a hypothetical chip with 40 960 modular multipliers at 1 GHz and 3 TB/s of HBM3, the example machine the FHE accelerator SoK uses. This is a model, not a measurement:

N = 2^16, 24 limbs of 64 bits; hypothetical chip: 41 T modmul/s, 3 TB/s HBM
dnum alpha=k  NTTs   modmults  key size mults/byte  compute  load key  bound
   1      24   144      198 M     48 MB        3.9    4.8 us    16.8 us  memory x3.5
   2      12   144      164 M     72 MB        2.2    4.0 us    25.2 us  memory x6.3
   3       8   160      163 M     96 MB        1.6    4.0 us    33.6 us  memory x8.5
   4       6   180      170 M    120 MB        1.4    4.1 us    41.9 us  memory x10.1
   6       4   224      193 M    168 MB        1.1    4.7 us    58.7 us  memory x12.5
  12       2   364      279 M    312 MB        0.9    6.8 us   109.1 us  memory x16.0
  24       1   650      463 M    600 MB        0.7   11.3 us   209.7 us  memory x18.5

What the table says:

  • One key switch is about 160–200 million modular multiplications and needs a 50–600 MB key, depending on dnum.
  • It is memory-bound at every setting. The chip can do 41 T modmul/s but only receives 3 TB/s, so it needs at least 13.7 multiplications per byte loaded to keep busy. Key switching offers 0.7–3.9.
  • dnum is a dial: fewer digits mean a smaller key and less traffic, but a bigger special modulus, which costs security budget (fewer levels for the same N). More digits mean more NTTs and much bigger keys.
  • This matches the literature. ARK's analysis (via the SoK) found CoeffToSlot at N = 2¹⁶, L = 23, dnum = 4 takes 0.18 ms of compute but 2.1 ms to load its data (11.7×).

Bootstrapping needs dozens of distinct rotation keys. That is gigabytes of key material per bootstrap if nothing is cached. Our toys show the same imbalance at miniature scale:

toy CKKS: 9 switching keys x 2 polys x 16 coeffs x 1829 bits = 64 KB (a ciphertext at level 0: 0.29 KB)
toy TFHE: bootstrapping key 64 KB, key-switching key 42 KB (an LWE ciphertext: 68 bytes)
toy BFV : one Galois key 7.5 KB (a ciphertext: 1.9 KB)

In every scheme, keys are 10–1 000× larger than the data.

4.3 The three ways out#

Each one raises the multiplications-per-byte ratio, and every serious accelerator combines them:

Trick Effect in the model (dnum = 3) Who
Generate the random half of each key on chip from a seed (it is pseudorandom) traffic halves: memory-bound ×8.5 → ×4.2 CraterLake (KSHGen unit), BTS
Reuse a key for many operations while it sits on chip (hoisting, multi-hop rotations that use one key repeatedly, operation scheduling) 8 uses per load: 33.6 µs / 8 = 4.2 µs ≈ compute time, i.e. balanced ARK ("inter-operation key reuse"), hoisted rotations in software
Large on-chip memory, so the working set never leaves the chip traffic → ~0 for keys that fit F1 (64 MB scratchpad), then larger on-chip storage in its successors; Heracles (64 MB on chip plus 48 GB HBM)

The same logic applies to the plaintext constants of bootstrapping's linear transforms, which can also run to hundreds of MB. ARK's "on-the-fly residue extension" regenerates their limbs on chip instead of loading them.

5. CPUs#

  • Libraries (OpenFHE, SEAL, HElib, Lattigo, TFHE-rs) use multi-threading across limbs and ciphertexts, plus SIMD for NTTs and modular arithmetic.
  • Intel HEXL accelerates NTTs and element-wise modular arithmetic with AVX-512, including the IFMA instructions (52-bit integer multiply-add). Vendor material reports roughly 2–5× on supported hardware.
  • A CPU core has a few hundred GB/s of memory bandwidth at best, and tens of MB of cache. It is far behind on both of §4's axes. Typical CKKS bootstrapping takes seconds on a multi-core CPU, and a TFHE PBS takes milliseconds per core.

6. GPUs#

GPUs are the practical accelerator in 2026. They are available everywhere, with thousands of integer lanes and TB/s-class HBM.

What maps well: the NTT's N/2 independent butterflies per stage, element-wise limb arithmetic, and running thousands of independent TFHE bootstraps at once.

What fights back:

  • GPUs have no native 64-bit modular multiply, so it must be emulated. Hence 32-bit designs like Cheddar.
  • Kernel launch overhead and intermediate results going through global memory are handled with kernel fusion, as in "100×" (Jung et al., TCHES 2021: GPU CKKS bootstrapping more than 100× faster than a single CPU thread).
  • Memory capacity for keys, and the cache hierarchy, are the focus of Theodosian (2025).
  • Using tensor cores for NTTs (TensorFHE) means reformulating the NTT as small matrix products.

Numbers (as reported by the authors; order of magnitude):

System Reported
FIDESlib (CKKS, 2025) bootstrapping ≥ 70× faster than AVX-optimized OpenFHE (up to 227.8× vs non-AVX OpenFHE)
Cheddar (CKKS, 32-bit) 2.9–25.6× over earlier GPU libraries; ResNet-20 inference in 1.50 s on an A100
Theodosian (CKKS, RTX 5090) bootstrapping 32 768 complex slots in 22.1 → 15.2 → 12.8 ms; 1.45–1.83× over Cheddar
TFHE-rs CUDA (TFHE) PBS < 1 ms on an H100; ~189 000 PBS/s on 8 × H100 (vendor figures)

7. FPGAs#

FPGAs offer custom datapaths with HBM, but limited on-chip memory (BRAM/URAM), lower clocks, and DSP blocks sized for small multiplies. Examples are HEAX, HEXL-FPGA, FAB and Poseidon (the last two with bootstrapping). For one logistic-regression iteration, the SoK lists Poseidon at 73 ms and FAB at 103 ms, against 775 ms for the "100×" GPU implementation. FPGAs are a good prototyping and niche-deployment target, and Niobium and others have used them on the way to ASICs.

8. ASICs: the academic lineage#

All of these are simulated designs: RTL synthesized for area and power, with performance modelled. None was fabricated.

Design Venue Key idea Notable (as reported)
F1 MICRO 2021 First programmable FHE accelerator: 16 clusters of 128-wide vector units (NTT, modmul, automorphism), 64 MB scratchpad, crossbars Up to ~17 000× over a CPU on LoLa-MNIST. But max N = 2¹⁴ and fixed dnum = L+1, so no fully packed CKKS bootstrapping
BTS ISCA 2022 "Bootstrappable" from the start: 64 × 32 grid of PEs, each with a butterfly and a base-conversion unit, local scratchpads wired to HBM Successor of the "100×" GPU work, with GPU-like organization
CraterLake ISCA 2022 Unbounded depth (N = 2¹⁶, depth up to 60), a 2048-wide vector processor (8 × 256), a CRB base-conversion unit, and KSHGen, which generates key halves on chip gmean 4 600× over CPU, 11.2× over the best prior accelerator at similar area and power
ARK MICRO 2022 Algorithm–hardware co-design: runtime data generation, multi-hop rotations so one key serves many rotations, hybrid limb/coefficient parallelism 6.5× faster than BTS on ResNet-20, where bootstrapping is 76 % of the work
SHARP ISCA 2023 36-bit words: smaller multipliers and memories, with precision managed in software
CiFHER, Osiris, … 2023–2026 chiplets (resizable systems), systolic arrays, memory-hierarchy-centric designs

The arc is clear: F1 proved the architecture, BTS and CraterLake made bootstrapping the design target, and ARK showed that changing the algorithm to fit the memory system beats adding more arithmetic.

9. Intel Heracles: from simulation to silicon#

Heracles is Intel's FHE accelerator from DARPA's DPRIVE program (2021–), presented at ISSCC in February 2026. It is the first widely reported fabricated large FHE accelerator. From press coverage (the ISSCC paper itself was not available to me, so treat the details as secondary):

Aspect Reported
Process / size an Intel 3 nm-class FinFET process; one report gives > 200 mm² (single-sourced)
Compute a grid of specialized compute tiles (one lower-authority source: 64 cores in an 8 × 8 SIMD grid)
On-chip memory 64 MB scratchpad plus register files and buffers; ~9.6 TB/s tile-to-tile interconnect
Off-chip memory 48 GB HBM3 in two stacks, ~819 GB/s
Speed 1 074–5 547× over a 24-core Xeon across seven FHE operations (one outlet gives the upper bound as 1 547×; sources conflict). Demo: an encrypted voter-verification query in 14 µs instead of 15 ms
Status research prototype, no announced commercial plans

Read through §4, the design makes sense. Off-chip bandwidth (~0.8 TB/s) is far lower than the on-chip interconnect (~9.6 TB/s), so performance depends on keeping keys and intermediate data on chip and reusing them. That is the CraterLake/ARK lesson, implemented in silicon. The 48 GB of HBM is there for capacity, holding the gigabytes of evaluation keys, more than for bandwidth.

Startups and others: Niobium (a DPRIVE spin-out; $23M raised; reported Samsung Foundry 8 nm deal), Cornami (reconfigurable fabric, working with DESILO on the GL scheme), Optalysys (photonic computing for the NTT/FFT), and Fabric Cryptography (07 §4).

10. TFHE hardware#

TFHE is a different workload: small ciphertexts, one PBS per gate or lookup, and a PBS that is n sequential external products (TFHE deep dive §4.3).

  • Latency per PBS is bounded by that sequential chain.
  • Throughput comes from running many independent PBS at once, which GPUs do well. Dedicated designs (e.g. Matcha, Strix, Morphling, and the FPGA design FPT in the academic literature) pipeline the external products and use FFT/NTT units tailored to N = 1024–2048.
  • The bootstrapping key (tens of MB) is reused by every PBS. It fits the "keep the key on chip" pattern better than CKKS's many rotation keys.

11. Algorithm–hardware co-design: where things are heading#

  • Shorter words (32–36 bits) with more limbs: cheaper multipliers, more parallelism.
  • Generate, don't load: seeded key halves, on-chip twiddle generation, regenerated plaintext limbs.
  • Reuse: hoisted rotations (decompose once, rotate many times), baby-step giant-step, multi-hop rotations, operation scheduling by compilers.
  • Hardware-shaped schemes: the Gentry–Lee matrix scheme turns encrypted linear algebra into plain matrix multiplication, which suits tensor cores and systolic arrays. MRFHE batches the DFT steps of bootstrapping. The CRYPTO 2026 shallow-bootstrapping work targets parallel hardware explicitly (07 §1–2).
  • Compilers as the interface: HEIR and similar compilers lower one program to CPU, GPU or accelerator backends. Google's 2026 HEIR demos reportedly involved hardware partners.

13. Exercises#

  1. 4-step NTT: rewrite NTT.forward as column NTTs, a twiddle multiplication, a transpose and row NTTs, and check that the results match.
  2. Montgomery multiplication: implement it for 30-bit primes and count integer multiplications per modmul against % p.
  3. Plug the NTT into a toy: replace poly_mul in toy_tfhe.py with an NTT over a prime Q and re-profile. How much faster is a PBS, and what dominates now?
  4. Hoisted rotations: in toy_ckks.linear_transform, decompose the input once and reuse the digits for all rotations. Count the multiplications saved.
  5. Seeded keys: store only a seed for the a half of each switching key in the toys, and measure how much key material is left.
  6. Tune dnum: extend the model with the security cost of the special modulus (larger PQ needs larger N) and find the best dnum for L = 24.

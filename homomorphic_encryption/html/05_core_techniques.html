05 · Core techniques: what makes FHE practical#

← 04 Schemes · index · next: 06 Security →

The schemes in 04 are the engines. This chapter covers the techniques that took FHE from "a billion times slower" to "deployed in iOS". Most performance wins come from data layout and algorithm choice, not from faster primitives.

1. Batching / SIMD packing#

By the CRT (ch. 02 §4), one plaintext polynomial holds many independent values ("slots"):

  • BGV/BFV with prime \(t \equiv 1 \pmod{2N}\): N slots of integers mod t (Smart–Vercauteren 2010/2014).
  • CKKS: N/2 complex slots.

Homomorphic + and × then act slot-wise, like SIMD vector instructions. One ciphertext multiplication performs 16 384 or 32 768 multiplications at once, which amortizes the enormous per-operation cost. Throughput-oriented FHE is all about keeping slots full.

2. Rotations (Galois automorphisms)#

Slot-wise operations alone can't move data between slots. The ring automorphisms \(\sigma_k : X \mapsto X^k\) (with k odd) permute the slots. With \(k = 5^j\) they act as a cyclic rotation by j (the slots form a 2 × N/2 hypercube; \(k = -1\) swaps the two rows, and for CKKS it is complex conjugation).

Applying \(\sigma_k\) to a ciphertext yields one under the key \(\sigma_k(s)\), so a key switch brings it back. Each rotation amount needs its own Galois key, or you compose power-of-two rotations.

Example: sum of all slots in \(\log_2(\#\text{slots})\) rotate-and-add steps:

[a b c d e f g h]
+ rot 4 → [a+e b+f c+g d+h  e+a ...]
+ rot 2 → [a+c+e+g  b+d+f+h ...]
+ rot 1 → [Σ Σ Σ Σ Σ Σ Σ Σ]            (every slot now holds the total)

Dot product = slot-wise multiplication + sum of slots. Rotations cost a key switch each, so minimizing rotations is a big part of FHE algorithm design.

3. Packing strategies for linear algebra#

How you lay data out in slots determines how many rotations you need:

  • Matrix × vector, diagonal method (Halevi–Shoup 2014): store the matrix by its generalized diagonals, so \(Mv = \sum_i \mathrm{diag}_i(M) \odot \mathrm{rot}_i(v)\).
  • Baby-step giant-step (BSGS): reduces rotations from d to about \(2\sqrt{d}\). It is used everywhere, including inside CKKS bootstrapping's linear transforms.
  • Matrix × matrix: Jiang–Kim–Lauter–Song (2018) and many successors. This is the bottleneck of encrypted transformers, and the motivation for the GL matrix scheme (ch. 04).
  • Convolutions: multiplexed packing for CNNs (e.g. Lee et al., ResNet-20 under CKKS with bootstrapping, 2022).
  • Column vs row vs "replicated" layouts, and layout conversion between layers, which is a major job of FHE compilers (§9).

4. Polynomial approximation of non-linear functions (CKKS / BFV)#

BGV/BFV/CKKS natively compute only polynomials. Everything else is approximated on a known, bounded input range:

Function Typical approach
Sigmoid, tanh, GELU, exp Chebyshev or minimax polynomials of moderate degree
ReLU, max, comparison, sign Composite minimax polynomials (Lee–Lee–Kim–No et al.): compose low-degree polys to approximate sign(x) sharply. ReLU(x) = x·(1+sign(x))/2
1/x, 1/√x (softmax, LayerNorm) Newton / Goldschmidt iterations from an initial polynomial guess
Softmax exp approx. + sum (rotations) + inverse approx. Often the hardest layer in encrypted transformers

Paterson–Stockmeyer evaluates a degree-d polynomial with about \(\sqrt{2d}\) non-scalar multiplications at depth \(\lceil \log_2 d \rceil\). The cost is set by depth: higher degree means better accuracy but more levels, and therefore more bootstrapping.

The alternative is to change the model: train with FHE-friendly activations such as \(x^2\) or low-degree polynomials, or quantize. The 2026 SoK on encrypted LLMs calls frameworks that do not change the model "model-preserving". Only 5 of 21 surveyed frameworks are (ch. 07).

5. Programmable bootstrapping and LUTs (TFHE)#

In TFHE every bootstrap evaluates a lookup table on an encrypted small integer (ch. 03 §8):

  • Any function on 1–8 bit inputs costs one PBS, regardless of how non-linear it is.
  • Bigger integers: radix decomposition (blocks plus carry propagation) or CRT decomposition (residues mod small coprimes; each block is independent and parallel).
  • Multi-value bootstrapping evaluates several LUTs on the same input in one blind rotation.
  • Tree-based / WoP-PBS (without padding) lift the 1-bit "padding" restriction caused by the negacyclic ring.

Zama's Concrete compiler turns Python/NumPy into TFHE circuits by choosing bit widths and placing PBS automatically. Concrete ML applies this to quantized ML models (trees, linear models, small NNs).

6. Functional bootstrapping in CKKS#

The "discrete CKKS" line (2024–2026) evaluates LUTs on many values at once inside CKKS bootstrapping, combining TFHE's expressiveness with CKKS's batching. Main papers: bootstrapping bits (Eurocrypt 2024), small integers / SI-BTS (Asiacrypt 2024), general functional bootstrapping (Alexandru–Kim–Polyakov), modular reduction, and high-precision discrete CKKS for noise flooding (2025). See ch. 07 for details.

7. Scheme switching#

Convert between CKKS/BFV (batched linear algebra) and FHEW/TFHE (cheap non-linear LUTs) as needed (ch. 04). It is powerful, but the switching itself is expensive, and its IND-CPA-D security has recently been shown to be subtle (ch. 06).

8. Multi-party variants#

Plain FHE has one key owner. For data from several parties:

Variant Idea Used by
Threshold FHE The secret key is secret-shared among n parties. Decryption needs t of them (a small MPC protocol) Zama Protocol (threshold decryption network), Lattigo multiparty, OpenFHE
Multiparty HE (MHE) Parties jointly generate one collective public key. Each keeps a share of the secret key (Mouchet–Troncoso-Pastoriza–Bossuat–Hubaux 2021) federated analytics, medical consortia (Tune Insight)
Multi-key FHE Each party has its own key. Ciphertexts under different keys combine on the fly (López-Alt–Tromer–Vaikuntanathan 2012) research. The cost grows with the number of parties

Threshold decryption needs noise flooding of the partial decryptions, or the key shares leak. This connects to IND-CPA-D in ch. 06.

Deep dive: deep_dives/threshold_fhe.md builds distributed key generation, 2-of-3 threshold decryption with smudging, and re-encryption on the toy BFV (code/threshold_bfv.py), and maps them onto the Zama Protocol.

9. Compilers and toolchains#

Writing FHE by hand means choosing parameters, placing relinearizations, rescales and bootstraps, designing slot layouts and approximating functions. Compilers automate this:

Tool Target Notes
HEIR (Google) multi-scheme, MLIR-based General-purpose HE compiler. Packing, parameter selection, arithmetization. Backends include OpenFHE and Lattigo. Paper: arXiv 2508.11095. Promoted by Google for encrypted AI inference in 2026
Concrete / Concrete ML (Zama) TFHE Python → TFHE circuits. Quantized ML models
Orion CKKS PyTorch → CKKS for deep CNNs, automated bootstrapping placement (ASPLOS 2025)
Fhelipe CKKS Tensor-compiler approach (PLDI 2024)
EVA / CHET (Microsoft) CKKS Early (2019–2020) CKKS compilers, scale management
Google FHE Transpiler TFHE / BGV C++ → boolean circuits (2021). Superseded by HEIR

Compilers are seen as a key enabler. HEIR in particular aims to be a common layer between front ends (PyTorch, Python) and both software libraries and hardware accelerators. Google's 2026 demos reportedly involved hardware partners such as Niobium.

10. Bandwidth tricks#

  • Seeded ciphertexts: for symmetric encryption, the uniform part a can be regenerated from a PRNG seed, which halves upload size.
  • Transciphering / hybrid HE: the client encrypts with a cheap symmetric cipher (AES, or HE-friendly ciphers such as Rasta, Pasta, Kreyvium, Elisabeth). The server holds an FHE encryption of the symmetric key and homomorphically decrypts to obtain FHE ciphertexts. It gives a tiny upload at the cost of server work.
  • Ciphertext compression / packing LWE → RLWE for responses (common in PIR).

11. Integrity and verifiability#

FHE gives confidentiality, not correctness. Approaches to verifiable FHE include SNARK/STARK proofs of correct homomorphic evaluation (an active 2023–2026 research area; the proofs are heavy because FHE arithmetic is large), redundancy or replication across servers, and running the FHE evaluator inside a TEE for "belt and braces". Zama's protocol, for example, combines ZK proofs that inputs were encrypted correctly with several independent coprocessors that recompute results and must agree.

02 · Mathematical foundations: lattices, LWE and Ring-LWE#

← 01 Introduction · index · next: 03 How it works →

Gentle version: primer P7 Lattices and LWE builds up learning with errors (LWE) and Ring-LWE from noisy linear equations, with hand-worked numbers and runnable code.

Every practical fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) scheme today rests on one hard problem, Learning With Errors, usually in its ring form. This chapter builds up the minimum mathematics needed to read the scheme descriptions.

1. Lattices in one page#

A lattice is the set of all integer combinations of some basis vectors \(b_1, \dots, b_n \in \mathbb{R}^m\):

\[\mathcal{L}(B) = \{\, B z \;:\; z \in \mathbb{Z}^n \,\}\]

Think of an infinite, regular grid of points, possibly very skewed. The same lattice has many bases. A good basis has short, nearly orthogonal vectors. A bad basis has long, nearly parallel ones.

The hard problems:

  • SVP (Shortest Vector Problem): find the shortest non-zero lattice vector.
  • CVP / BDD (Closest Vector / Bounded Distance Decoding): given a point near the lattice, find the closest lattice point.
  • Approximate versions: γ-SVP asks for a vector within a factor γ of the shortest; GapSVP is the decision version (is the shortest vector shorter than d, or longer than γ·d?); SIVP asks for n linearly independent vectors that are all short.

With a good basis, CVP is easy: round off coordinates (Babai's algorithm). With a bad basis in high dimensions it is believed to be infeasible, even for quantum computers. The best known algorithms (lattice sieving, enumeration, BKZ (block Korkine–Zolotarev) reduction) run in time exponential in the dimension.

2. Learning With Errors (LWE)#

Introduced by Oded Regev (2005), who won the 2018 Gödel Prize for it.

Fix a dimension n, a modulus q, and a "small" error distribution χ (e.g. a discrete Gaussian with σ ≈ 3.2). Pick a secret \(s \in \mathbb{Z}_q^n\). An LWE sample is

\[(\,a,\ b = \langle a, s\rangle + e \bmod q\,), \qquad a \leftarrow \mathbb{Z}_q^n \text{ uniform},\ e \leftarrow \chi\]
  • Search-LWE: given many samples, find s.
  • Decision-LWE: distinguish LWE samples from uniformly random pairs (a, b).

Why the error is everything. Without e, each sample is a linear equation \(b = \langle a, s\rangle\), and Gaussian elimination recovers s from n samples. Adding a little noise to each equation turns an easy linear-algebra problem into a lattice problem (BDD in the lattice generated by the a's) that is believed to be hard.

Worst-case hardness. Regev proved that solving LWE on average (random instances) is at least as hard as solving approximate SVP / SIVP in the worst case on any lattice, via a quantum reduction. Peikert (2009) and Brakerski et al. (2013) later gave classical reductions. This is a much stronger guarantee than RSA (Rivest–Shamir–Adleman) has: there, average-case hardness of factoring is simply assumed.

Tiny example (n = 3, q = 17, secret s = (2, 5, 11)):

a = (1, 4, 9)    <a,s> = 2 + 20 + 99 = 121 ≡ 2 (mod 17)    e = +1   → b = 3
a = (6, 0, 13)   <a,s> = 12 + 0 + 143 = 155 ≡ 2 (mod 17)   e = −1   → b = 1
...

Given only the (a, b) rows, recover s. With n = 3 you can brute-force it. With n = 1024 and well-chosen q, σ, no known algorithm can.

LWE as an encryption scheme (preview of chapter 03)#

Encode a message m ∈ Z_t into the high-order bits of b, where the noise can't reach it:

\[c = (a,\ b = \langle a, s\rangle + \Delta m + e), \qquad \Delta = \lfloor q / t \rfloor\]

Decryption computes \(b - \langle a, s\rangle = \Delta m + e\) and rounds away e. By decision-LWE, b looks uniformly random, so m is hidden. Adding two ciphertexts adds the messages and adds the noises. That is the seed of homomorphism.

3. Ring-LWE: the efficient version#

Plain LWE is inefficient: a key or ciphertext involves an n × n matrix's worth of random a's, and operations cost \(O(n^2)\). Ring-LWE (Lyubashevsky–Peikert–Regev, 2010) replaces vectors with polynomials.

The ring used by nearly all FHE schemes is

\[R = \mathbb{Z}[X] / (X^N + 1), \qquad R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[X] / (X^N + 1), \qquad N = 2^k\]

Elements are polynomials of degree < N with coefficients mod q. Multiplication is ordinary polynomial multiplication followed by the rule \(X^N = -1\), which wraps coefficients around with a sign flip ("negacyclic convolution"). \(X^N + 1\) is the \(2N\)-th cyclotomic polynomial, and this choice gives both nice algebra and fast arithmetic.

Example in \(\mathbb{Z}_{17}[X]/(X^4+1)\):

(1 + 2X + X³) · (3 + X²)
  = 3 + 6X + X² + 2X³ + 3X³ + X⁵
  = 3 + 6X + X² + 5X³ + X·X⁴          and X⁴ = −1, so X⁵ = −X
  = 3 + 5X + X² + 5X³

An RLWE sample is \((a,\ b = a \cdot s + e) \in R_q^2\), where s and e are polynomials with small coefficients. One RLWE sample "contains" N correlated LWE samples, so:

  • keys and ciphertexts shrink from \(O(N^2)\) to \(O(N)\) numbers;
  • multiplication costs \(O(N \log N)\) with the number-theoretic transform (NTT, below);
  • the ring structure enables packing many values into one ciphertext, so one operation acts on all of them (single instruction, multiple data: SIMD batching, ch. 05).

The cost is extra algebraic structure that an attacker might exploit. No such attack is known for the power-of-two cyclotomics used in FHE: the best attacks on RLWE are the same as on LWE.

Module-LWE sits in between (small matrices of ring elements). It is the basis of the post-quantum standards of NIST (the National Institute of Standards and Technology): ML-KEM (Kyber, a module-lattice-based key-encapsulation mechanism) and ML-DSA (Dilithium, a module-lattice-based digital signature algorithm), but FHE schemes mostly use RLWE or plain LWE. FHE and post-quantum cryptography share the same hardness foundation.

4. The Number Theoretic Transform (NTT)#

The NTT is the fast Fourier transform (FFT) over a finite field, and it is the computational kernel of FHE.

If q is prime with \(q \equiv 1 \pmod{2N}\), then \(\mathbb{Z}_q\) contains a primitive \(2N\)-th root of unity ψ. The N roots of \(X^N + 1\) are then \(\psi, \psi^3, \psi^5, \dots, \psi^{2N-1}\), and by the Chinese Remainder Theorem

\[\mathbb{Z}_q[X]/(X^N+1) \;\cong\; \underbrace{\mathbb{Z}_q \times \cdots \times \mathbb{Z}_q}_{N \text{ copies}}, \qquad a(X) \mapsto \big(a(\psi), a(\psi^3), \dots, a(\psi^{2N-1})\big)\]

In this "evaluation form", polynomial multiplication becomes pointwise multiplication. The NTT and its inverse take \(O(N \log N)\), so a ring multiplication costs \(O(N \log N)\) instead of \(O(N^2)\).

Profiles of FHE libraries are dominated by NTTs and by key switching, which is built on NTTs. This is why FHE hardware accelerators (ch. 07) are mostly NTT engines plus a lot of memory bandwidth. A runnable NTT, measured against schoolbook multiplication, is in the hardware deep dive §2.

The same CRT idea over \(\mathbb{Z}_t\) (the plaintext modulus) gives batching in BFV and BGV (the Brakerski/Fan–Vercauteren and Brakerski–Gentry–Vaikuntanathan schemes). Over \(\mathbb{C}\) it gives the canonical embedding of CKKS (the Cheon–Kim–Kim–Song scheme). One isomorphism appears three times.

5. RNS: making 1000-bit moduli fast#

Ciphertext moduli are huge. With \(N = 2^{16}\), \(\log_2 q\) can be over 1500 bits, and arithmetic on 1500-bit integers is slow. The fix is the Residue Number System:

\[q = q_1 \cdot q_2 \cdots q_L, \qquad q_i \text{ distinct NTT-friendly primes of } 30\text{–}60 \text{ bits}\]

A polynomial mod q is stored as L polynomials mod \(q_i\) (CRT again). All arithmetic then runs on machine words, in parallel across the "RNS limbs". Dropping a prime from the chain implements modulus switching and CKKS rescaling cheaply (ch. 03). Every modern library (SEAL, OpenFHE, Lattigo, HEaaN…) uses RNS variants of the schemes, often called "full-RNS" BFV or CKKS.

6. Parameters, security and sizes#

The security of (R)LWE depends on N (dimension), q (modulus) and the noise and secret distributions. A larger q makes attacks easier, since the noise is relatively smaller. Each N therefore admits a maximum q. These are the HomomorphicEncryption.org standard bounds for 128-bit classical security with a ternary secret, which SEAL and OpenFHE use as defaults:

N (ring dimension) max \(\log_2 q\) (128-bit) Roughly enough for
1 024 27 toy
2 048 54 a couple of additions
4 096 109 1–2 multiplications
8 192 218 ~4–5 multiplications
16 384 438 ~10 multiplications
32 768 881 ~20 mults, or bootstrapping with few levels left
65 536 ~1 700 comfortable CKKS bootstrapping

(The N = 65536 row is extrapolated from library practice. The other rows are the standard's table values. Concrete security is estimated with the Lattice Estimator of Albrecht et al., which models primal, dual and hybrid attacks.)

Size example. With \(N = 2^{14}\) and \(\log q = 438\), a fresh ciphertext is two polynomials: \(2 \cdot 16384 \cdot 438\) bits ≈ 1.8 MB. With batching it holds 8192 CKKS values or 16384 BFV slots, so the per-value expansion is much smaller than it first looks. Evaluation keys (relinearization, rotation, bootstrapping) can add hundreds of MB to several GB. Moving them through memory is a major bottleneck.

Secret distributions. Libraries use ternary secrets (\(\{-1, 0, 1\}\) coefficients), and sometimes sparse secrets (few non-zeros) because they make bootstrapping cheaper. Sparse secrets enable hybrid attacks, so parameters must account for them. The CRYPTO 2026 paper by Jain–Lin–Liu–Saha exploits sparse secrets to speed up FHEW/TFHE bootstrapping (the Ducas–Micciancio scheme and Torus FHE; ch. 07).

7. Why this is post-quantum#

  • Shor's algorithm breaks factoring and discrete log, and with them RSA, ElGamal, Paillier and pairing-based HE.
  • No quantum algorithm is known to solve LWE / RLWE much faster than classical ones. The best quantum speedups for lattice sieving are modest constant-in-the-exponent improvements.
  • In April 2024 Yilei Chen posted a claimed polynomial-time quantum algorithm for LWE with certain parameters. Within about ten days a bug was found and the claim was withdrawn. The episode was a useful stress test, and it showed the community scrutinizes these assumptions hard.

Caveat specific to FHE: circular security. Bootstrapping publishes an encryption of the secret key under itself. Security of that is an extra assumption ("circular security") that is not implied by LWE, although no attack is known. Leveled schemes can avoid it in theory by using a fresh key per level, as Brakerski–Vaikuntanathan and BGV did. Practical libraries use a single key, though, so their relinearization and rotation keys (encryptions of s² or σ(s) under s) already rely on a mild circular-type assumption.
